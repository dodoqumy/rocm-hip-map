## 摘要

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers replace the feed-forward block of a Transformer with E expert networks, and each token is routed to k of these experts. Under expert parallelism (EP) the experts are distributed across GPUs, and every MoE layer runs all-to-all collectives in the forward and backward passes to dispatch tokens to their experts and then combine the results. On a cluster with 8 AMD Instinct MI300X GPUs per node, these collectives can take 45% of the training step at EP32 with top-2 routing and 60% with top-6 routing. We find that early in pretraining routers have already learned to assign tokens to experts in correlated patterns, both within a layer and across layers. At top-2, 0.8% of the expert pairs in a layer are selected together by 42% of tokens, and the experts a token selects at one layer predict the experts it selects at the next layer. We use these correlations to keep more token--expert assignments on the token's own GPU, which reduces communication across GPUs and across nodes. Correlated expert placement puts experts that are often selected together on the same GPU. Combined with a dispatcher that sends each token to each GPU once, it removes up to 58% of dispatched rows. Token shuffling applies when sequence parallelism shards tokens across the EP group. It moves each token to the GPU predicted to hold its next-layer experts during the reduce-scatter that follows attention. On one node this raises the share of token--expert assignments served on the token's GPU from 12.5% to 59%. In Megatron-LM, across EP degrees from 8 to 64 with top-2 and top-6 routing, the two methods reduce all-to-all time by 1.16-2.63X and end-to-end step time by up to 1.41X. Neither method changes the models' underlying routing decisions or expert parameters.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers have become a central building block of large language models [Jiang et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2025, Kimi Team, 2026]. In an MoE layer, the single feed-forward network (FFN) of a dense Transformer block is replaced by a set of E parallel FFNs, called experts, together with a lightweight router [Shazeer et al., 2017, Lepikhin et al., 2021, Fedus et al., 2022]. After the attention sub-layer, the router scores each token’s hidden state against all experts and selects its top-k experts. The token is processed only by those experts, and their outputs are combined using the router weights. Model capacity therefore grows with E, while the FLOPs per token grow only with k. Because each expert is updated only by the tokens routed to it, experts become specialized over the course of pretraining [Dai et al., 2024, Xue et al., 2024]. A second line of work exploits expert affinity for MoE inference. GRACE-MoE [Han et al., 2025] groups experts that are often selected together within a layer onto the same GPU, first across nodes and then across the GPUs of each node, and sends a token to each GPU only once. Its groups differ in size, and it replicates heavily loaded experts to rebalance the GPUs, which costs memory. We apply within- layer grouping to pretraining with equal-size groups and no replicas. ExFlow [Yao et al., 2024] uses inter-layer expert affinity to co-locate experts across layers. This approach does not transfer directly to pretraining. During autoregressive decoding, a token can be placed on any convenient GPU. During pretraining, a token stays on the GPU that holds its part of the sequence for the attention sub-layer, and moving it requires additional communication. Our starting point is that once experts specialize during pretraining, routing follows stable patterns. We study expert routing across pretraining stages and load-balancing methods, and find two such patterns. The first is within-layer correlation. Some pairs of experts are selected together much more often than independent routing would predict. After approximately 2.1 B training tokens with top-2 routing, 42 % of tokens select one of only 0.8 % of the possible expert pairs, and this pattern is already present after about one billion tokens. The second is cross-layer correlation. The experts a token selects at layer ℓpredict the experts it selects at layer ℓ+1. With token shuffling added, A2A time is reduced by 1.40×–1.74× under top-2 and by 1.46×–2.63× under top-6.

## II. MOTIVATION

A. All-to-All Cost in MoE Pretraining We begin by measuring how much of an MoE training step is spent in the dispatch and combine collectives. Each MoE layer runs both collectives in the forward pass and again in the backward pass. The cost of these collectives depends on two configuration choices. A larger expert-parallel degree spreads experts over more GPUs, so the fraction of a token’s experts on its own GPU falls as 1/EP. Beyond a single node, however, the exchange also moves from the intra-node fabric to the inter-node network. A larger top-k raises the number of activation rows each token contributes to both collectives, so top-6 moves three times the volume of top-2. We exploit these correlations to raise token–expert locality, the fraction of (token, selected expert) pairs whose expert is on the token’s GPU. These pairs never enter the network, so higher locality reduces the total A2A volume. Our methods require no architectural changes or expert replicas, and they do not change routing decisions or the training loss. Correlated placement and deduplication apply to any layout that uses expert parallelism. Token shuffling applies only to layouts that use tensor parallelism with sequence parallelism and in which the TP group and the EP group are the same set of GPUs (TP = EP), such as TP8 EP8 and TP16 EP16 in our evaluation. It does not apply to layouts without tensor parallelism (TP1). Correlated expert placement and deduplica- tion also apply to inference, where affinity-based grouping has already been evaluated [Han et al., 2025]; we do not evaluate inference. We measure a 12-layer MoE model (dmodel=4096), with 128 experts of FFN width 1024 per layer (see Section VI and Table II for the detailed configuration). Figure 1 shows that the two effects compound. We measure all-to-all time as the wall-clock time the step spends inside the dispatch and combine calls, which includes time spent waiting for other GPUs. Widening the EP group from a single node (EP8) to four nodes (EP32) raises the all-to-all share of step time from 13.5 % to 44.8 % under top-2 and from 24.0 % to 60.4 % under top-6. At fixed EP8, moving from top-2 to top-6 raises it from 13.5 % to 24.0 %. At EP32 with top-6, the two collectives take more of the step than expert computation and all non-MoE computation combined. We make the following contributions: • Correlated Expert Placement and a Deduplicating Dispatcher (Section V-A). We place correlated experts on the same GPU, so that the experts a token selects are more likely to share a GPU. The deduplicating dispatcher sends each token to a destination GPU at most once, regardless of how many of its selected experts are there. Deduplication is an existing technique [Zhao et al., 2025]. The all-to-all dispatcher in Megatron-LM [Shoeybi et al., 2019] sends one copy of a token per selected expert, so we implement deduplication there as the basis for placement. Together, placement and deduplication reduce both intra-node and inter-node communication volume. B. Expert Correlation Within and Across Layers Crucially, the measurements above use contiguous place- ment, which ignores the correlations between the experts a token selects. Once experts have specialized, these selections follow two regular patterns, which we exploit in this paper. We measure both on the top-2 and top-6 models from Section II-A. All statistics in this section are computed offline. We record every token’s expert choices at every MoE layer on a few late training steps (131k tokens per step), fit each placement and predictor on some of these steps, and apply them to the recorded routes of the other steps without running the dispatcher. • Token Shuffling (Section V-B). For sequence-parallel layouts in which the TP group equals the EP group, we (i) predict a token’s experts at layer ℓ+1 from the experts the token selected at the previous layers, and (ii) move the token ahead of time to the GPU that is predicted to hold those experts. The move is folded into the reduce- scatter that sequence parallelism [Korthikanti et al., 2023] already performs after attention. This brings to pretraining the locality benefit that prior affinity-based work obtained for inference. a) Within a Layer, Experts Are Selected in Stable Pairs: Figure 2a shows, for layer 8, the probability that a token selecting expert e1 also selects e2. Expert Parallelism When the experts of a layer no longer fit in the memory of one GPU, expert parallelism (EP) partitions them across a group of GPUs, typically alongside data parallelism. With E=128 experts and EP=8, for example, each GPU hosts E/EP=16 experts. ## V. IMPLEMENTATION

A. Within-Layer Correlation: Correlated Expert Placement B. Hybrid Parallelism: Tensor–Expert Parallelism As discussed in Section II-B and shown in Figure 2a, some experts within a layer are frequently selected together by the same tokens. We use this structure to reduce all-to-all (A2A) communication with two techniques. Correlated expert placement puts frequently co-selected experts on the same GPU, and a deduplicating dispatcher sends each token to each EP rank at most once. For large models and long-context training, expert paral- lelism alone is not enough to fit the model in GPU memory, and additional model parallelism is needed. Tensor parallelism (TP) [Shoeybi et al., 2019] shards the computation of each layer across GPUs and is widely used in both pretraining and inference. In a common hybrid TP–EP configuration, attention and the MoE layer use different token layouts. Attention is partitioned across the TP ranks, and the MoE layer operates on sequence shards distributed across the same ranks [Korthikanti et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2025, Singh et al., 2023]. Two collectives move between these layouts. After attention, a reduce-scatter sums the partial TP outputs and partitions the sequence, which leaves each rank with a contiguous subset of tokens. These tokens then pass through the dispatch all-to-all, the expert computation, and the combine all-to-all. After the MoE layer, an all-gather reconstructs the full sequence on every TP rank for the next Transformer block. Figure 3 shows the A2A communication for three con- figurations: the baseline, with deduplication, and with both deduplication and correlated expert placement. 1 shows contiguous placement with the conventional dispatcher, where each (token, expert) assignment produces a separate A2A row. When a token selects several experts on the same destination rank, its hidden state is sent there several times. 2 adds deduplication. Assignments that share the same (token, destination rank) are merged, so the hidden state is sent to that rank once. 3 adds correlated expert placement, which puts frequently co-selected experts on the same rank. This increases token–expert locality and gives deduplication more rows to merge. Neither technique changes the router’s expert assignments or the expert computation. Token shuffling replaces this reduce-scatter and all-gather with shuffled versions (Figure 5), and it requires the TP group and the EP group to be the same GPUs (TP = EP). The shuffled reduce-scatter sends each token to the rank predicted to hold most of its experts in the coming MoE layer, based on the experts it selected in the previous layers. The shuffle- aware all-gather restores the canonical token order before the next attention layer. A larger fraction of each token’s experts is then on its own GPU, and the dispatch and combine all-to-alls send fewer bytes across the network. Section V-B describes the mechanism in detail. Experts are not tensor-parallel in this work. Some latency-sensitive inference deployments shard experts with tensor parallelism, which we do not consider. a) Deduplicating Dispatcher: Conventional top-k dis- patch sends one copy of the token’s hidden state per (token, expert) assignment, even when several selected experts reside on the same EP rank. We instead send one row per unique (token, destination rank) pair. Each row carries the IDs and routing probabilities of the token’s experts on that rank. The receiving rank uses this metadata to give each of its selected experts a copy of the token and to group the copies by expert for the expert computation, so no separate collective is needed for this metadata. The combine path works the same way. The expert rank weights the outputs of the token’s experts by their routing probabilities, sums them, and returns one partial output per (token, rank) pair. Dispatch and combine therefore send one row per token for each rank that holds at least one of its experts. Deduplication is an existing technique [Zhao et al., 2025], and we implement it in Megatron-LM as the basis for our correlated placement algorithms.

At top-6 the gains are 1.08× at We showed that expert routing in MoE pretraining is cor- related both within a layer and across layers, and that these correlations form within about one billion tokens and then stay stable. Correlated expert placement uses the within-layer correlation to put co-selected experts on the same GPU, and a deduplicating dispatcher sends each token at most once to its destination GPU. Token shuffling uses the cross-layer correlation to move each token to the GPU predicted to hold its next experts, subject to per-rank capacity constraints. Moreover, this shuffle occurs inside the reduce-scatter that sequence parallelism already performs, leading to almost no overhead. Neither method changes routing decisions or expert parameters. On a cluster of nodes with eight MI300X GPUs each, the two methods reduce all-to-all time by 1.16–2.63× and end-to-end step time by up to 1.41×, with the largest gains at top-6 when the EP group spans several nodes. Our evaluation covers one 12-layer model with top-2 and top- 6 routing, trained for 2.1B tokens. Specifically, our implementation modifies three components. (i) Token dispatcher: We add a deduplicating variant of the all-to-all dispatcher that sends one row per unique (token, destination rank) pair instead of one per (token, expert) pair. Expert IDs and gate weights are carried with the activation, and the receiver expands the rows into expert-major order. The combine direction applies the same deduplication in reverse. (ii) Expert placement: We extend the dispatcher’s contiguous expert-to-rank assignment to support an arbitrary per-layer permutation. The permutation is applied to the routing map, and checkpoint expert weights are keyed by global expert ID, allowing the same checkpoint to be loaded with a different expert placement. (iii) Token shuffling: For TP=EP layouts, we modify attention’s sequence-parallel reduce-scatter to assign each token to an owner rank based on the second-order affinity table. Canonical token order is restored around attention, the output layer, and the loss. The route IDs the planner needs are included in the dispatcher’s existing metadata all-gather, requiring no additional collective. Routing decisions used to build the offline expert placement and affinity tables are recorded through a capture hook in the router. All variants train the same model and produce the same loss, differing only in how tokens are placed and moved across ranks. B. Ablation Over Routing Strategies a) Load-Balancing Methods: Our methods depend on the router producing correlated expert choices. To test how the load-balancing method affects this, we trained two more top-6 models that differ from the main top-6 model only in the balancing method. The first uses a sequence-level auxiliary loss ten times stronger than the main model’s (coefficient 10−1 instead of 10−2). It gives the most balanced expert load of the three, with a maximum-to-mean load ratio of 1.20. The second uses the auxiliary-loss-free method of DeepSeek-V3 [Liu et al., 2024], which adds a per-expert bias to a sigmoid router. Its load ratio is 1.37, and it reaches the lowest loss of the three (4.39, against 4.46 for the main model and 4.64 for the stronger auxiliary loss). We evaluate the same configurations on all three models. b) Gains Follow Each Router’s Co-Selection Structure: Correlated placement remains effective across all three routing strategies. With correlated placement, deduplication removes 58 %, 51 %, and 47 % of dispatched rows at EP8 for the main model, the stronger auxiliary-loss router, and the auxiliary-loss-free router, respectively, and 49 %, 40 %, and 36 % at EP16. In comparison, contiguous placement removes only about 25 % at EP8 and 13 % at EP16 for every router (Figure 11, right). These reductions translate into consistent end-to-end gains across routing strategies: at TP1 EP16, step speedups are 1.41×, 1.30×, and 1.26×, respectively, and remain at least 1.20× on every EP16 layout (Figure 11, left). Thus, the benefit of correlated placement does not depend on a particular routing strategy; its magnitude instead follows the co-selection structure produced by each router. Load balance affects the realized communication time because an all-to-all must wait for the most heavily loaded rank. At EP16, for example, the main model removes 49 % of dispatched rows compared with 40 % for the stronger auxiliary-loss router, but its speedup advantage is smaller (1.41× versus 1.30×), consistent with its higher load ratio (1.52 versus 1.20). Correlated placement and deduplication therefore target communication volume through expert co-selection, rather than communication skew. Methods that directly reduce skew, such as relocating or replicating heavily loaded experts [Zhai et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2026, Han et al., 2025], address a separate source of all-to-all cost and are complementary to our approach.
