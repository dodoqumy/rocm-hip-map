## 摘要

Modern GPU-based HPC systems rely on heterogeneous vendor stacks, yet resilience studies are largely limited to single architectures, leaving it unclear how faults behave across different GPU backends. We present \emph{BitIR}, a cross-architecture fault injection framework that injects deterministic single-bit faults at the LLVM IR level, ensuring semantically equivalent perturbations prior to backend lowering and enabling direct cross-vendor comparison across NVIDIA, Intel, and AMD GPUs. We evaluate BitIR on three production supercomputers -- Polaris (ALCF, NVIDIA A100), Aurora (ALCF, Intel GPU Max 1550), and Frontier (OLCF, AMD Instinct MI250X) -- representing the full spectrum of current leadership-class GPU architectures. Using representative heterogeneous benchmarks, we conduct large-scale injection campaigns across all three systems and classify outcomes into masked results, silent data corruptions (SDCs), and failures. Our results show that identical faults produce markedly different behaviors across vendors: Intel most often masks faults but exhibits more hangs when faults escape masking, NVIDIA exposes more detectable hard failures, and AMD alternates between SDC-dominant and failure-dominant behavior depending on the benchmark and fault site. These findings demonstrate that resilience is not backend-invariant, underscoring the need for backend-aware and workload-aware fault mitigation.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) are now central to mod- ern high-performance computing (HPC) and AI systems. As these platforms continue to scale, they become increasingly vulnerable to transient faults caused by radiation, voltage noise, and timing variation [1], [2]. Although hardware protec- tion mechanisms mitigate some of these effects, many faults still propagate to software-visible state and can ultimately alter application behavior. In heterogeneous applications, where GPUs execute the dominant computational kernels, even a single bit perturbation can lead to silent corruption, abnormal termination, or stalled execution [3], [4]. Unlike many prior GPU fault-injection studies that rely on statistical or randomized sampling of fault locations for tractability [8], [9], [10], [11], BitIR exhaustively evaluates the complete aligned injection space for each benchmark. This is particularly important for cross-vendor comparison, where sparse sampling can miss backend-specific divergence concentrated in specific workloads, instruction classes, or site categories. Understanding how GPU applications behave under tran- sient faults is, therefore, an important resilience problem. This is especially true for scientific and data-intensive workloads, where incorrect outputs may be difficult to detect and where GPU kernels can fail silently without OS-level signals or reliable log evidence. ## III. METHODOLOGY

Given a heterogeneous application that offloads compute- intensive kernels to GPUs, our goal is to systematically characterize how single-bit faults affect application resilience across NVIDIA, Intel, and AMD GPU architectures under a common fault model. To enable fair and consistent cross- platform comparison, our methodology enforces deterministic execution, output-equivalent validation, and precise alignment of injected sites across vendor backends. Figure 1 illustrates the overall workflow. Each site is annotated with several attributes. The site class captures the role of the site in the IR. Result sites model corruption of a computed value, operand sites perturb an instruction input, and pointer sites corrupt a device memory address, typically via a getelementptr chain. The value type determines the bit width and semantic interpretation of the perturbation. Integer sites cover indices, counters, control-flow conditions, and arithmetic results, floating-point sites appear in arithmetic kernels, where exponent flips shift values by orders of magnitude and mantissa flips introduce bounded relative error, and pointer sites hold memory addresses whose corruption may redirect loads or stores to invalid regions, producing outcomes from silent overwrite to access violation. Additional attributes, such as opcode and function context are recorded to support cross-platform site alignment. At the core of BitIR is the LLVM intermediate represen- tation (IR)[15], which serves as a unifying software-visible abstraction prior to backend lowering. LLVM IR is typed, static single assignment (SSA)-based, and widely used in modern CPU and GPU compilation pipelines before vendor- specific code generation. Unlike backend binaries, LLVM IR preserves instruction structure, operand relationships, and type information, allowing faults to be defined in terms of program semantics rather than hardware-specific encodings. Using LLVM IR and its debug metadata, BitIR constructs de- terministic site/bit worklists, applies aligned injections across platforms, and classifies outcomes through comparison against golden outputs. Worklist Construction. For each enumerated site, BitIR constructs a worklist of (site_id, bit_index) pairs, where the bit index ranges over all bit positions of the target value for each site (e.g., 32 positions for float and typically 64 for pointers on modern GPU backends). Each sitebit pair uniquely identifies a single fault injection experiment. These pairs serve as primary keys in both the worklist and the resulting per-run output artifacts. Cross-architecture equivalence is essential to the validity of our study. We define it as follows: equivalence holds if, for all platforms, each fault injection satisfies (i) input equivalence — identical program inputs and output comparison criteria; (ii) fault equivalence — the same bit position is perturbed in a semantically equivalent LLVM IR object across all backends; and (iii) interpretation equivalence — results are classified and aggregated under a common methodology. We validate the reproducibility and stability of this method- ology by regenerating site inventories and confirming identical IDs, bit widths, and metadata across runs on unchanged IR. Injection Execution. Given a (site_id, bit_index) pair, the injector locates the target IR object, bitcasts it to an integer of matching width, applies an XOR with a one- hot mask at the specified bit position, and bitcasts the result back to its original type, guaranteeing a single-bit flip that preserves type correctness. This mechanism allows faults to be introduced uniformly across different LLVM IR-visible targets while keeping the perturbation semantics reproducible. A. Golden Reference Generation For each application, we first establish a golden reference output by executing the fault-free benchmark with hooks that emits a deterministic binary snapshot of its final program state. This includes output buffers, result arrays, or other application-specific outputs for each benchmark. We verify that all expected outputs are generated and non-empty, then repeat the execution and compare the resulting snapshots for bitwise equivalence. Additional fault-free baseline executions were performed during campaign validation and consistently reproduced the corresponding golden references. This verifi- cation procedure ensures that we are able to produce stable, deterministic outputs for each benchmark that can be used for output classification throughout injection campaigns. BitIR records a shared metadata schema for each candidate site, including target class, opcode, type, bit width, operand index, function context, source location, and a signature ordi- nal that disambiguates sites sharing the same source location. These attributes are combined into what we refer to as a semantic identifier that characterizes the LLVM IR object independently of any vendor-specific profiling or numbering schemas. To maintain cross-platform alignment, sites with semantic identifiers that do not map cleanly across vendor compilation flows are pruned. Only 15 of 1150 total injection sites (1.3%) were removed from the aligned benchmark set. These sites correspond to backend or compiler-specific helper machinery, including CUDA builtin-variable helpers, shuffle intrinsics, and device-function wrappers introduced by Clang CUDA support headers. These sites represent a negligible fraction of candidate sites and preserves near-complete coverage of the aligned injection space. No injection runs were executed for pruned injection sites. B. LLVM IR-Level Fault Injection Fault injection is performed at the LLVM IR level under a unified fault model through three steps: site enumeration, worklist construction, and injection execution. Site Enumeration. We traverse the device-side LLVM IR of each application to identify eligible injection sites. A site is defined as any LLVM IR-visible target for which a fault can be introduced. ## V. RESILIENCE ANALYSIS

A. BitIR Implementation We structure our analysis around five research questions tar- geting distinct dimensions of cross-architecture fault behavior. BitIR is a multi-component LLVM-based GPU fault injec- tion framework built on LLVM 17.0.0 [15] and integrated with CUDA, SYCL, and HIP toolchains. It consists of three com- ponents: (1) an LLVM pass plugin (libfi_inject.so) for IR-level fault injection, implemented in C++; (2) backend compilation flows, orchestrated via Bash, that lower injected IR through vendor-specific toolchains into executable binaries; and (3) a Python-based campaign execution layer that manages site inventory generation, worklist construction, cross-platform alignment, result aggregation, and integration with the sched- ulers. Integer and control-bearing instructions such as xor, select, shl, and udiv also contribute strongly on NVIDIA and AMD while remaining much more often masked on Intel. These trends show that SDC risk is highly structured rather than uniform across backends, and that the same backend can be SDC-heavy on one workload and failure- heavy on another. D. During large-scale campaign execution on Aurora, segmentation faults caused nodes to go offline and trigger system maintenance, prompting direct contact from the ALCF operations team. SASSIFI [9] operates at the NVIDIA SASS level, NVBitFI [10] extends this model with dynamic binary instrumentation, and gpuFI- 4 [11] further refines campaign design and site classification. These systems are effective for real-hardware studies, but their reliance on backend-specific representations prevents natural alignment of equivalent faults across CUDA, HIP, and SYCL toolchains. Recent cross-layer studies [22], [23] make this limitation even clearer by showing that resilience conclusions can shift materially with the abstraction level and methodology used for injection. A second line of work similar to BitIR provides the compiler and application level foundation for our approach. LLFI [24], FlipIt [25], and REFINE [26] establish the viability of IR-level fault injection for CPU-based HPC workloads, showing that LLVM IR can support both portability and meaningful propa- gation analysis. GPU-oriented extensions move this reasoning closer to our setting. LLFI-GPU [27] brings LLVM based FI to GPU applications, Li et al. [28] study software-visible GPU error propagation, and GPU-Trident [29] develops efficient models of GPU error propagation. Related application- and compiler-level studies [30], [6], [31] further show that program structure, data representation, compiler transformation, and execution context can significantly affect resilience. These works justify LLVM IR as a meaningful analysis layer, but none establishes a unified framework that uses aligned LLVM IR fault sites to drive directly comparable campaigns across NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel GPUs on production systems.

## VII. RELATED WORK

Prior work on GPU resilience spans architectural, microar- chitectural, software, and compiler-level perspectives. Foun- dational architectural studies establish the fault-outcome con- cepts and evaluation principles underlying resilience analy- sis [1], [5]. On GPUs, early vulnerability studies show that fault manifestation depends strongly on both microarchitec- tural location and application behavior. Tan et al. [3] analyze soft-error vulnerability in GPGPU microarchitecture, while Vallero et al. [7] compare reliability behavior across NVIDIA and AMD GPUs. More broadly, prior evidence comes from both modeled or simulation-based analyses and field studies of deployed systems. Radiation-induced and large-scale system studies [2], [4], [20], [21] reinforce the same conclusion that GPU fault outcomes vary substantially with hardware structure, workload, and deployment environment. Together, these studies establish why GPU resilience must be studied empirically, but they do not provide a portable mechanism for injecting semantically aligned faults and observing their manifestation through real application runs across vendor ecosystems. Many prior GPU fault injection studies rely on statistical or randomized sampling of fault locations for tractability [8], [9], [10], [11], which is a practical design choice for large campaigns but can miss structured divergence concentrated in specific opcodes, site classes, or workload contexts. For cross- vendor comparison, this limitation is especially important. If aligned faults are sampled sparsely, backend-specific differ- ences may be underrepresented or missed entirely. BitIR eval- uates the complete aligned injection space for each retained benchmark rather than doing any sampling, preserving fine- grained comparability across vendors. The methods most directly related to BitIR begins with software-visible GPU fault injection. GPU-Qin [8] is among the earliest frameworks for systematic GPU resilience evalua- tion through software-level fault injection.
