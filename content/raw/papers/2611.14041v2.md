## 摘要

The time to solution of an iterative solver depends on the number of iterations and the cost per iteration. This paper develops algorithms that reduce both for elliptic problems discretized with high-order continuous finite elements on distributed GPUs, implemented in a unified matrix-free code supporting CUDA, HIP and SYCL. A distributed direct fast-diagonalization (FD) Poisson solver provides exact solutions on tensor-product meshes with direction-wise grading and coefficients varying along one coordinate direction. Its tensor data remain distributed, moved between slab layouts using collective transposes or kept in place by a Schur-complement approach. A two-level hybrid Schwarz preconditioner with FD cell solves (HS-FD) is extended to 2:1 hanging-node octrees with exact local cell inverses, while a p-multigrid preconditioner with Chebyshev--Jacobi smoothing (CJ-pMG) resolves hanging-node constraints at every level within fused kernels. Both approaches exploit FP32, where cell shapes and coefficients allow, within an FP64 conjugate-gradient iteration, adaptively select replicated or distributed coarse levels, support block solves for multiple right-hand sides and determine all parameters from the operator without case-specific tuning. Comparisons with Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate gradients (Jacobi PCG), MFEM, nekRS and deal.II span uniform, graded and octree meshes, layered coefficients, and four applications across NVIDIA, AMD and Intel GPUs. The direct solver is two to three orders of magnitude faster than Jacobi PCG on uniform meshes. HS-FD converges in 11 iterations for the generalised Poisson problem on octree meshes across 8 to 1024 MI250X graphics compute dies. These results provide practical guidelines for solver selection based on mesh structure and coefficient characteristics.

The finite-element (FE) method handles complex geometries and locally refined meshes, and its accuracy improves systematically as the mesh is refined and the poly- nomial degree is raised. This work uses continuous (C0) Lagrange elements of poly- nomial degree p from 2 to 7 to discretize the underlying elliptic operators, and solves the resulting sparse system by a preconditioned Krylov method, whose iterations are dominated by the operator action and the preconditioner application. At high degree the operator action is evaluated matrix-free by sum factorization, and fused kernels make it efficient on GPUs [24]. Most preconditioners for sparse linear systems, including Gauss–Seidel relaxation, incomplete factorizations and algebraic multigrid (AMG), are built from the entries of the assembled sparse matrix. A matrix-free code never forms this matrix, so these preconditioners are not directly available to it. AMG can still be applied to the sparse matrix of a simpler operator, such as a low-order-refined one [38], but its setup then adds to the cost. Assembling the sparse matrix of the degree-p discretization instead needs far more memory (about 100 GB for 1.1 × 107 degrees of freedom (DoFs) at p = 7). A matrix-free preconditioner is therefore built mainly from what the operator kernels already compute: the operator action, the diagonal of the operator and the tensor-product structure of the shape functions. From the operator action, the diagonal and the tensor-product structure, we design three solvers, one from each of three families, and order them by how much of the inverse of the operator they apply: the diagonal, exact cell inverses or the exact inverse of the whole operator. The p-multigrid preconditioner CJ-pMG applies the diagonal as a Chebyshev–Jacobi smoother on every level of a hierarchy of polynomial degrees. Every level runs on the GPU in the sum-factorized kernel at a lower degree on the cells and partition of the fine mesh, so an adaptively refined octree needs no mesh hierarchy and no repartitioning. The price is a trilinear coarse level on the fine mesh, replicated on every MPI rank except when BoomerAMG solves more than 106 unknowns or FD solves it on the Aurora 3D-IC stacks (subsection 4.1). The two-level hybrid Schwarz preconditioner HS-FD applies on every cell the exact inverse of a cell problem by fast diagonalization (FD). Where the neighbors of a cell have its size, the cell problem is the restriction of the operator to the cell. It remains a Kronecker sum of one-dimensional operators next to hanging nodes and for a coefficient fitted by a separable function (subsection 4.4 and section SM11). The direct solve by FD applies the exact inverse of the whole operator in the same way. It requires the whole operator to be a Kronecker sum, as for a coefficient that is constant or varies along one direction, discretized on a tensor-product mesh, graded and fully periodic ones included. Each method in this order inverts more of the operator and needs more of its tensor structure. FD is the established local solver of Schwarz methods for spectral elements because one FD solve costs about as much as one operator action, so an iterative local solver would have to converge in a single iteration to compete [27]. This argument motivates FD here, in HS-FD and as a direct solve on the whole mesh. The three solvers are compared with Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate gradients (Jacobi PCG), the solver of the CEED bake-off problems [14], in the same code on the same meshes, right-hand sides, tolerance and GPUs. They are also compared with established solvers in three other codes: low-order-refined AMG in MFEM, the p-multigrid of nekRS and the host p-multigrid of deal.II (subsections 5.2 and 5.7). Published speedups come from different codes, machines, tolerances and meshes, so they do not tell which solver to prefer. This work asks which solver suits a distributed GPU code on a given operator and mesh, and where its advantage comes from. FD [28] is used in high-order element methods mostly as a local solver, in Schwarz methods and patch smoothers [27], and as a global solver on uniform meshes [25] or for second-order finite differences on graded grids. Costa et al. also apply it there on multiple GPUs [7]. High-order codes apply AMG only to the coarse problem of degree 1 [33] or to a low-order-refined (LOR) operator [38]. LOR with BoomerAMG runs on GPUs with hanging nodes, and for a screened Poisson problem at p = 6 on one V100 GPU about 75% of the time to solution is setup [39] (section SM7). The matrix-free p-multigrid and Schwarz preconditioners of these studies run on GPUs only on conforming meshes [13], on equidistant Cartesian meshes [8] or on a single GPU [24], and on hanging-node octrees only on CPUs [33]. Problem formulation. 2.1. Model operators and discretization. Three elliptic problems on a box Ω⊂R3 are considered: the Poisson equation, the generalized Poisson equation with a variable coefficient, and the screened Poisson equation, (2.1) −∇2u(x) = g(x), −∇· = g(x), −∇2u(x) + σu(x) = g(x), ϵ(x)∇u(x) where u(x) is the unknown, g(x) is the source, ϵ(x) ≥ϵmin > 0 is a scalar coefficient and σ > 0 is a constant. The screened Poisson equation arises when diffusion terms are integrated implicitly in time, with σ inversely proportional to the time step. The coefficient ϵ(x) comes from the application. In the test problems of section 5 it is the dielectric function of implicit solvation [16], the inverse density of bubbly flow [29], the conductivity of a fiber composite [32] or the tissue conductivity of a human head [19]. In several problems the coefficient varies along one direction only, ϵ(x) = κ(z), with κ constant on layers. Such a layered coefficient describes the earth in resistivity tomography [20], a layered Darcy medium or the stack of a three-dimensional integrated circuit (3D-IC) [17]. The integrals are evaluated with the tensor-product Gauss rule of nq = np points per direction, so the quadrature points do not coincide with the GLL nodes and the mass matrix is consistent (non-diagonal). The generalized Poisson equation is discretized in its divergence form, with ϵ evaluated at the quadrature points. Munch et al. use the same rule, with p + 1 Gauss points per direction, in their matrix-free multigrid [33]. The CEED bake-off problems use p+2 Gauss points, or the p+1 GLL nodes themselves as in spectral-element codes [14]. On the box-shaped cells used here, the rule computes the stiffness and mass matrices exactly where the coefficient is constant on each cell. Where ϵ varies or jumps inside a cell, it is sampled at the quadrature points. Constraints and the singular periodic case. A hanging node is a weighted sum of its constraining nodes on the coarser neighbor, a periodic constraint copies the value from the constraining to the constrained node, and a homogeneous Dirichlet node is zero. One constraint matrix C maps the free DoFs ˆu (the uncon- strained nodes, constraining nodes included) to all nodes, u = Cˆu, and testing (2.2) with the free DoFs gives the system that is solved, (2.3) CT AC ˆu = CT f. C and its transpose are applied cell-locally inside the operator action, and from here on A, K and M denote CT AC, CT KC and CT MC on the free DoFs. With periodic conditions in all three directions and σ = 0, the matrix K is singular, and its null space is spanned by the vector of ones, 1. The system then has a solution only if 1T f = 0, so the mean of the entries of f is subtracted from f. The solution is unique up to an added constant, which each method fixes in its own way. The direct solve of section 3 applies a pseudoinverse that returns the zero- integral solution, Ωuh dx = 1T Mu = 0. Inside CG the constant is removed from the preconditioned residual by a projection weighted by the diagonal of the operator, the metric-matched projection. This weight matches the Jacobi and Chebyshev–Jacobi preconditioners, and for CJ-pMG and HS-FD it leaves the iterations unchanged. Direct solve by fast diagonalization. On a tensor mesh with a constant coefficient, the discrete operator of the Poisson and screened Poisson equations (2.1) is a sum of Kronecker products of one-dimensional stiffness and mass matrices, and fast diagonalization (FD) applies its inverse exactly [28]. For high-order elements FD has been used mostly as a local solver, and globally only on uniform meshes, on one GPU or for finite differences (subsection 1.1). The Kronecker structure, the exact inverse, the treatment of the null mode and the transposes of the distributed solve follow known constructions [28, 7]. This work adds the direct FD solve of the high- order FE operator on graded meshes on distributed GPUs, with O(N/P) memory per MPI rank and blocks of right-hand sides, extends it to a coefficient that varies along one direction (section SM8), and uses it as the exact coarse solve of both multigrid preconditioners (section SM9). 3.1. Kronecker-sum structure. On a tensor mesh, every global shape function is a product of one-dimensional FE shape functions, ϕijk = ϕx k, and the Gauss quadrature rule of subsection 2.1 is a tensor product as well. Every integral arising in (2.2) then factorizes into one-dimensional integrals, and the operator A = K + σM, with σ ≥0, takes the form (3.1) K = Kx⊗My⊗Mz+Mx⊗Ky⊗Mz+Mx⊗My⊗Kz, M = Mx⊗My⊗Mz, where Kd, Md ∈Rnd×nd are the one-dimensional stiffness and mass matrices along direction d ∈{x, y, z} and nd is the number of free nodes along d. They are assembled over the nd e cells along d, each with its own size he, so grading a direction changes only the pair (Kd, Md). The mass matrices are consistent, so FD diagonalizes each pair by a generalized eigenproblem Kds = λMds (subsection 3.2) and inverts the discrete operator itself, with no mass lumping, to rounding error. The structure survives the constraints of (2.3) whenever C = Cx ⊗Cy ⊗Cz, as for homogeneous Dirichlet and periodic conditions, which act on one direction at MATRIX-FREE SOLVERS FOR HIGH-ORDER FINITE ELEMENTS 7 a time. A Dirichlet direction removes its two end nodes, so nd = nd ep −1 and Kd is symmetric positive definite. A periodic direction identifies its last node with its first, so nd = nd ep, the pair (Kd, Md) gains entries in its top-right and bottom-left corners, and Kd has a simple zero eigenvalue with the constant vector as eigenvector. At hanging nodes a line of nodes crossing a refinement interface has more free nodes on the fine side than on the coarse side, so the free DoFs are not a Cartesian product, and FD does not apply to the whole operator. Robin conditions with a positive coefficient constant on whole faces add it to the end diagonal entry of Kd and keep the structure (section SM10), but the experiments use Dirichlet and periodic directions. A coefficient that does not separate into one- dimensional factors, such as the coefficient of the octree problems of section 5, breaks the Kronecker structure, and the problem is then solved by CG. 3.2. A solve applies the three mode products by ST d , scales every entry by 1/Λijk and applies the three products by Sd (Figure 1(a), Algorithm SM2.1). The entry map moves the locally owned DoFs into k-slabs, where the products along x and y are applied. A transpose moves the tensor to i-slabs for the products along z and the scaling. A second transpose and the exit map return the result to the locally owned DoFs. The solver of Costa et al. uses such collective transposes on GPUs [7]. If the FE cell partition matches the k-slabs, the entry and exit maps reduce to local copies. After the products along x and y, the solve along z splits into one banded system per line along z. For a block of nv right-hand sides, each mode product becomes one matrix product with nv times as many columns, and each exchange carries all nv vectors in one message. A replicated scheme, which all-gathers the right-hand side and solves redundantly on every rank, is slower than the distributed one on two or more MPI ranks (section 5). Cost per iteration. The time to solution of a preconditioned Krylov method is written as the number of iterations times the cost of one iteration, (5.1) Tsolve = nit (a top + tpre + tvec + tcomm) , where nit is the number of iterations and a the number of fine-level operator actions per iteration. The four times in (5.1) are recorded per iteration. The time top of one fine-level operator action includes its ghost exchange, and tpre covers the rest of the preconditioner (intermediate and coarse levels, grid transfers and local box solves, with their ghost exchanges). The vector updates of CG take tvec, and the reductions take tcomm: the inner products of CG and, if present, the coarse-residual sum. The four times in (5.1) depend on the implementation and the machine. The first three scale with N/P, while tcomm is bound by latency and grows with P. The setup time Tsetup (eigenvalue bounds, coarse operators, one-dimensional eigenproblems) is reported separately, because it is spent once per mesh and shared by all its solves. Table SM5 gives the work and the cost of one iteration. On the octree mesh H- GP on 32 A100 GPUs, CJ-pMG needs 257 to 258 times fewer iterations than Jacobi PCG, while each of its iterations costs 25.1 times more (10.5 against 0.418 ms). Its CG solver time is therefore 10.2 times shorter (0.0946 against 0.969 s). The intermediate levels, the transfers and the replicated coarse solve with its global reduction contribute the remaining factor of about 1.9. HS-FD needs 11 iterations on this operator, and one of its iterations costs 5.47 ms on Polaris and 4.42 ms on Frontier, 0.52 and 0.62 times a CJ-pMG iteration, against 0.418 and 0.319 ms for Jacobi PCG. On the ERT problem the direct FD solve is 82 to 256 times faster than LOR-AMG (section SM24). 6. Conclusions. This paper develops three solvers for high-order elliptic prob- lems in one matrix-free code on distributed GPUs, written in CUDA, HIP and SYCL. The direct FD solve is distributed, keeps only its share of the tensor on each MPI rank and also serves as an exact coarse solve. It needs an operator that is a Kronecker sum, which requires a tensor-product mesh and a coefficient that varies along one direction only. HS-FD is extended to 2:1 hanging-node octrees with exact cell inverses, and CJ- pMG resolves the hanging-node constraints of every level inside fused kernels. Both preconditioners use FP32 inside FP64 CG, choose a replicated or a distributed coarse level, solve blocks of right-hand sides and take every parameter from the operator. Where the operator is a Kronecker sum, one FD solve replaces hundreds to hun- dreds of thousands of Jacobi PCG iterations, and its time does not depend on the mesh grading up to R = 4096. On the other problems both preconditioners need a number of iterations independent of h under uniform refinement, and HS-FD keeps its iterations low on strongly graded and octree meshes, with 11 on the octree gen- eralized Poisson problem on 8 to 1024 MI250X GCDs. CJ-pMG and HS-FD are 2.2 to 3.6 times faster than nekRS on the Poisson box in the time of the CG iterations. At 109 DoFs nekRS with a coefficient-aware multigrid is faster than HS-FD on the EEG problem on Aurora and the 3D-IC problem on Polaris, and slower on the 3D-IC problem on Aurora and Frontier. HS-FD is faster than LOR-AMG on every problem compared except the ERT problem, where the direct FD solve is faster than both. The measurements give a selection rule (Figure 5): the direct FD solve on a Kronecker sum, and otherwise CJ-pMG or HS-FD by iterations and cycle cost. The iterations of HS-FD grow with the FE degree, as two-level bounds for cell subdomains with minimal overlap predict [50, 4], and vertex patches, which avoid this growth, have no Kronecker form at a hanging interface. Both preconditioners smooth within one cell, so their iterations grow on stretched cells. For HS-FD the coarse level of BoomerAMG on the low-order-refined operator corrects this (subsection 4.4), for CJ-pMG the widened window only in part. Neither coarse space adapts to the coefficient, so isolated inclusions at a large coefficient ratio still slow HS-FD. A replicated coarse level costs one Allreduce per V-cycle and a coarse solve on every rank, and it grows with N, so an AMG coarse level of more than 106 unknowns is distributed. The FP32 parts switch to FP64 only at a cell aspect ratio or a ratio of the coefficient within one cell of 300, not at a coefficient jump between cells. The automatic selection, timed at setup, can choose differently on other runs or machines and assumes a stationary cycle. The tensor structure of the direct solve also holds for a separable coefficient a(x)b(y)c(z), for Robin faces (subsection 3.1) and for the shifted-Laplacian precondi- tioner of the indefinite Helmholtz operator, which FD inverts in complex arithmetic. 24 G. The fully periodic operator in the FD solve. For the fully periodic operator with σ = 0, the FD solve replaces the reciprocal of the single zero eigenvalue by zero, u = A+ Mf with A+ M of (3.5). In floating point the computed Λ000 is only close to zero, so the entry Λ+ 000 is set to zero by its index (0, 0, 0), not by comparing Λijk with a tolerance. Costa et al. [SM16] also set the corresponding modal unknown to zero, but detect it by a scale-aware tolerance on the vanishing pivot. Because the eigenbasis is M-orthonormal, A+ M is the pseudoinverse in the mass-matrix inner product (Proposition 3.1). Applied to a compatible right-hand side, it returns the zero-integral solution of subsection 2.2, and applied to an incompatible one, it solves the problem whose source has its mean value subtracted. The Euclidean Moore– Penrose pseudoinverse would instead return the solution with zero nodal sum, 1T u = 0, where 1 is the vector of ones. That solution differs from the zero-integral one by a constant unless M1 is a multiple of 1. In the k-slab layout the tensor is divided among the P MPI ranks along z, and rank r owns the planes kr 0 ≤k < kr 1. The lines along x and y are then local, and the products with Sx and Sy and their transposes need no communication. In the i-slab layout the tensor is divided along x, the lines along z are local, and the product with ST z , the scaling and the product with Sz are applied in that layout. A solve in the general case therefore takes four collectives: an entry map from the FE ordering of the locally owned DoFs into the k-slab, a transpose to the i-slab, a transpose back, and an exit map back to the locally owned DoFs. Each collective moves nxnynz/P values per rank, and the index lists of all four are exchanged once at setup, so a solve moves only field values. The entry and exit maps reduce to local copies when the cell partition of the FE code is aligned with the slabs, that is, when each rank owns exactly the cells whose nodes form its k-slab. Every direct FD solve in section 5 therefore uses a slab partition of the cells, except the solve of the fully periodic Poisson problem U-P on Aurora (Table 1). Slab partition. Rank r owns the cell layers l with ⌊rL/P⌋≤l < ⌊(r + 1)L/P⌋, where L is the number of cell layers along the slab direction. The slab direction, written z in subsection 3.4, is the non-periodic direction with the most cell layers, or a periodic one on a fully periodic mesh. The partition is built with the fully distributed triangulation of deal.II [SM5]. For the direct solve with more ranks than layers, the ranks form the two-dimensional grid described below. The CJ-pMG and HS-FD solves use the slab partition only on meshes that are not fully periodic and have L ≥P. Otherwise the cells keep the space-filling-curve partition of the octree mesh library [SM13], and the entry and exit maps of an FD coarse solve remain collectives. A slab has two full cross sections as its interface, so at large P the ghost exchange of the operator action moves more data per rank than with the compact subdomains of the space-filling-curve partition. Automatic parameter selection. Every preconditioner parameter fol- lows from values that the setup computes for the operator at hand, and a short measured trial decides where several settings are admissible. The upper ends of the Chebyshev windows, the Ritz-value lower end of the HS-FD window and the degree of the coarse Chebyshev iteration come from Lanczos estimates of the eigenvalues of the preconditioned operator. This section completes the review of subsection 1.1. On GPUs, libCEED [SM9] also evaluates operators without a matrix by sum factoriza- tion. Kronbichler and Wall [SM40] use Chebyshev–Jacobi smoothing in high-order multigrid, and Casarin [SM14] analyzes overlapping Schwarz methods for the con- forming spectral element discretization. Fast diagonalization and tensor direct solvers. Lynch, Rice and Thomas [SM45] diagonalized separable finite-difference operators through the eigenvectors of their one- dimensional factors. Farnell [SM23] computed these eigenvectors numerically for non- uniform grids. In high-order element methods, FD is mostly a local solver, for Schwarz subdomains [SM44, SM24] and patch smoothers [SM74, SM73, SM17]. As a global solver, FD has been applied to spectral elements on uniform rectangular domains on one GPU [SM41] and, through fast Fourier transforms, to uniform high-order FE meshes [SM76]. For second-order finite differences on graded grids it is applied as dense matrix–matrix products [SM38, SM16], and on multiple GPUs in [SM16]. Schwarz methods with FD local solves. Lottes and Fischer [SM44] use an over- lapping Schwarz method with a coarse-grid solve as the preconditioner for spectral elements on two-dimensional Cartesian meshes. Each local solve inverts, by FD, the operator restricted to one element extended by one layer of nodes of its neighbors. Because neighboring local solves overlap, each node is weighted by the inverse of the number of local solves that contain it. The weight is applied on both sides so that the preconditioner stays symmetric for CG. nekRS runs Chebyshev-accelerated Schwarz smoothers of this kind on GPUs for conforming meshes [SM62]. Nek5000 extends them to nonconforming meshes on CPUs [SM60], and Munch and Kronbichler use them within p-multigrid on CPUs [SM50]. Janssen and Kanschat [SM33] smooth adaptive meshes by multiplicative Schwarz on cell patches with exact local solves, and keep the hanging nodes out of the smoother by smoothing each level only on its refined cells. Vertex patches give iterations nearly independent of the polynomial degree, while cell patches with minimal overlap do not [SM11, SM70]. The matrix of a vertex patch is dense in the Lagrange basis, and factorizing it costs O(p9) operations in three dimensions. A sparse basis lowers this to the cost of an operator action [SM12]. FD inverts the patch exactly where its cells form a tensor-product grid [SM11, SM17], but neither approach treats hanging nodes. HS-FD uses cell patches, whose operators stay Kronecker sums next to hanging nodes, and like other cell patches with minimal overlap its iterations grow with the polynomial degree (subsection 5.2). Stopping test and reported accuracy. CG variants and blocks of right-hand sides. All preconditioners are applied inside the conjugate gradient method (CG). The CG builds on the GPU implementation of Kodali et al. [SM37], which keeps every vector on the GPU and applies the operator with the fused matrix-free kernels of subsection 4.2. In place of its standard recurrence with two reductions per iteration, we use the single-reduction variant of Chronopoulos and Gear [SM15], which computes the two inner products of an iteration in one global reduction. CJ-pMG and HS-FD run partly in FP32, so they are symmetric only up to FP32 rounding and vary slightly from one application to the next. For a block of right-hand sides, CJ-pMG and HS-FD solve all right-hand sides at once.
