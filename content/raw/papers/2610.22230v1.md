## 摘要

Meta deploys a heterogeneous fleet comprising NVIDIA GPUs, AMD GPUs, and proprietary MTIA accelerators to reduce costs and mitigate vendor lock-in. However, the extensive history of NVIDIA-specific optimizations hinders performance portability to other hardware. This paper presents our methods for optimizing alternative accelerators, using AMD Instinct MI350X versus NVIDIA B200 for recommendation model training as a case study. We propose three reusable principles. First, hardware-model co-design is essential: rather than directly porting models from NVIDIA GPUs, revising model structures and software recipes allows full exploitation of accelerator-specific strengths (e.g., MI350X's 1.6x HBM capacity relative to B200). Second, our analytical performance model enables zero-warmup optimal kernel selection across diverse datatypes and matrix shapes in production models, drastically simplifying tuning. Third, increasingly mature agentic workflows have lowered the barrier to porting from the CUDA ecosystem; we extend these workflows for microarchitecture-aware kernel optimizations and custom shim generation. Compared to the directly ported baseline, these methods deliver up to 1.5x improvement in end-to-end training throughput. To our knowledge, this is the first hyperscaler report on productionizing AMD GPUs for large-scale training. Our contributions have been upstreamed to the open-source PyTorch, Triton, and ROCm ecosystems.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The immense potential of AI applications has catalyzed an unprecedented expansion in the accelerator landscape. While NVIDIA remains the incumbent platform, the AMD Instinct™[1, 2] GPU has emerged as a viable alternative. Meanwhile, hyperscalers have also developed custom sili- con, including Meta MTIA [3–6], Google TPU [7], Amazon Trainium [8], Microsoft Maia [9], Huawei Ascend [10], Alibaba Hanguang [11], and Baidu Kunlun [12]. Capitalizing on this hardware proliferation, Meta deploys a heterogeneous fleet comprising NVIDIA GPUs, AMD GPUs, and proprietary MTIA accelerators at scale. This strategic diversification delivers several benefits: (1) enabling cost optimization by preserving the flexibility to adopt the most economical solutions as the market evolves; (2) strengthening supply-chain resilience; and (3) matching model characteristics to the most suitable hardware. Analytical performance model. Traditionally, selecting high- performance general matrix multiplication (GEMM) kernels relies on offline tuning—a cumbersome process that generalizes poorly across the disparate matrix dimensions common in DLRMs. We propose Origami, an analytical performance However, the extensive history of NVIDIA-specific opti- mizations hinders performance portability to other hardware. model that predicts optimal tile configurations in microseconds, achieving 93% accuracy relative to exhaustively autotuned kernels across diverse matrix shapes. While analytical models are not new, Origami extends prior approaches to multi-chiplet architectures, with accuracy validated at production scale. Agentic workflows for kernel optimization. To reduce en- gineering burden and migration friction, we codify complex kernel-tuning principles into agentic workflows: automating microarchitecture-aware configuration selection, swizzling pro- cess IDs, incorporating occupancy hints, and leveraging spe- cialized hardware instructions. Together, they achieve 1.6×– 2× speedups across both hand-written and compiler-generated kernels. Furthermore, we leverage agentic workflows to auto- mate the application of solutions that address hardware-specific bottlenecks; e.g., to mitigate the performance impact of atomics on MI350X, we apply a tree-reduction algorithm that utilizes shared memory to deliver up to 4× speedup in custom kernels. B200 offers 180 GB of HBM capacity, with 900 GB/s of scale-up bandwidth per GPU. • Synchronization and network pressure: Training involves numerous GPUs in tight lockstep with frequent synchroniza- tion, heavily straining network efficiency. B200 and MI350X are same-generation flagships with closely matching theoretical bounds: their near-identical peak compute (2250 vs. 2300 BF16 TFLOPS) and HBM bandwidth (8 TB/s) yield largely coincident roofline curves [27], and thus a similar performance envelope for ML workloads. Hipification. Hipification is AMD’s CUDA-to-HIP (Heterogeneous-compute Interface for Portability) translation process that converts NVIDIA CUDA code into HIP equivalents; see Fig. 2. There are two primary approaches: (1) compile-time hipification using AMD’s open-source hipify-clang compiler, which translates CUDA constructs to HIP at build time, and (2) rule-based source-code translation using hipify_torch, which traverses and rewrites source files before compilation. While hipification provides broad functional compatibility and enables most CUDA workloads to run on AMD with minimal manual effort, performance portability remains a challenge. For instance, CUDA uses a warp size of 32, whereas an AMD GPU uses a wavefront size of 64. Kernels that naively hardcode warp sizes will likely perform suboptimally without tuning. • Kernel and optimizer: Training requires comprehensive backward kernels and advanced optimizer support. • Generalization: While inference can be optimized per workload, training must rely on generalized optimizations for out-of-the-box performance—orders of magnitude more training trials run before a model is finalized, making per-job optimization infeasible. Addressing these challenges requires systematic optimizations across the software stack, from low-level libraries (Section III) and kernel tuning (Section IV) to high-level model co-design (Section V).

## LIBRARIES

The ROCm software platform provides optimized libraries, runtimes, and firmware for AMD GPUs. Grounded in roofline models [27] and hardware constraints, we optimize core GEMM kernels and selection algorithms to approach theoretical limits. To optimize RCCL, the collective communication library for distributed training, we derive theoretical bounds from hardware specifications as optimization targets. These roofline-driven prin- ciples enable low-level libraries to deliver robust, generalizable performance across diverse AI workloads. A. GEMM Kernels for AMD MI350X The emergence of agentic AI tools has fundamentally lowered the cost of switching from NVIDIA to AMD. At Meta, we leverage AI agents to address issues in the hipification process. DLRM workloads present a broad range of matrix shapes and sizes, including irregular dimensions that stress standard GEMM kernel selection strategies. To deliver strong performance across this diversity, we integrate performance enhancements into hipBLASLt [29] GEMM kernels through its assembly backend, TensileLite [30]. These enhancements leverage new CDNA 4 [31] hardware capabilities alongside algorithmic refinements that reduce the solution space and enable finer- grained runtime parameter selection via Origami (Section III-B). Beyond exploiting new ISA features, we employ the following algorithmic techniques. Stream-K partitions GEMM computations across CUs at finer granularity than traditional data-parallel tiling, improving load balance for tensor sizes that do not evenly tile the device. We also introduce new reduction strategies and runtime dispatch logic, improving robustness across a broad range of compute- and memory-bound tensor sizes. Notably, a single GEMM kernel can now achieve performance previously requiring multiple specialized kernels. Workgroup mapping via space-filling curves. MI350X GPUs use a multi-XCD architecture in which workgroups are dis- tributed across XCDs, each with a private L2 cache. Scheduling workgroups operating on adjacent or overlapping tiles across different XCDs degrades cache locality and efficiency. To address this, hipBLASLt introduces a mapping strategy based on space-filling curves that preferentially co-locates workgroups processing nearby tiles on the same XCD, improving spatial lo- cality, cache utilization, and power efficiency. Multiple mapping strategies are supported within the GEMM assembly kernels, with runtime-selectable parameters integrated with Origami to select the optimal strategy for a given tensor size, kernel configuration, and target hardware. For each candidate tile configuration with LDS tile size (MT , NT , KT ), Origami computes cycle counts for compute and memory operations, then selects the configuration minimiz- ing total latency. The compute latency for one tile is: Lcompute = NMI · LMI (1) where NMI = ⌈MT /MI⌉· ⌈NT /NI⌉· ⌈KT /KI⌉is the number of matrix instructions per tile (macro tile dimensions divided by instruction dimensions) and LMI is the architecture-specific instruction latency. Memory latency is computed by cascading traffic through the cache hierarchy. The bytes loaded per tile are: Bytestile = MT · KT · bA + NT · KT · bB (2) where bA, bB are bytes per element. Origami predicts cache hit rates by modeling workgroup sharing within each memory scope. For NCU CUs sharing a cache, arranged as an MC × NC grid of output tiles, the predicted hit rate is: H = 1 −MC · MT + NC · NT MC · NC · (MT + NT ) (3) This formula applies to L2 and LLC, with NCU set to the number of CUs sharing that cache scope. It captures data reuse: A tiles are shared across NC workgroups computing different output columns, while B tiles are shared across MC workgroups computing different rows. Tiles are rounded to the nearest cache line width in the contiguous dimension of the allocation (determined by transpose). The model constrains cache tile dimensions when working sets exceed cache capacity. Traffic reaching each memory level determines hierarchy latency. Issue Rate Bound. Effective memory throughput can be limited by the rate at which CUs issue loads and stores, not just bandwidth. This creates another parallelism-locality tradeoff on what are usually considered bandwidth-bound problems: smaller tiles increase the number of vector lanes issuing load/store requests but reduce data reuse, while larger tiles improve locality at the cost of issue rate. Origami models achievable throughput as a function of occupied vector lanes to navigate this tradeoff. Latency Model. Beyond tile configuration, Origami selects the optimal Stream-K grid size. E. Shampoo Optimizer and rocSOLVER Shampoo [35, 36] is a second-order optimizer widely de- ployed due to its faster convergence compared to first-order methods like Adam [37]. Shampoo maintains Kronecker- factored preconditioner matrices, requiring periodic compu- tation of matrix fourth roots to update the preconditioners. This matrix root computation relies on eigen decomposition provided by the rocSOLVER library. For large models, these steps dominate Shampoo’s overhead, making rocSOLVER’s performance critical to overall training throughput. When thousands of threads contend on a single address (as is common in reduction-style kernels), these coherency messages serialize execution, leading to poor performance. The fundamental issue is that per-thread global atomics scale poorly with thread count on multi-chiplet architectures. On a monolithic GPU, the L2 cache can coalesce nearby atomic operations; on a multi-chiplet GPU, each chiplet must coordinate with the others, and a single contended address becomes a serialization bottleneck. Our measurements show that MI350X can be over 10× slower than B200 on atomic-heavy kernels at high contention rates, while matching or exceeding B200 on atomic-free, bandwidth-bound kernels. Algorithmic strategies: hierarchical reduction and caching. We employ two complementary strategies depending on the contention pattern. When many threads contend on a single shared address (e.g., a global counter), we apply hierarchical reduction: threads accumulate results in fast on-chip memory (registers or shared memory), a parallel tree reduction sums them within each block, and a single representative thread issues one global atomic. This converts N global atomics into ⌈N/block_size⌉, a reduction factor of up to 1024×. When threads target many distinct addresses with temporal locality (e.g., gradient scatter to embedding rows), we apply caching: each thread maintains a small register cache of recently written addresses, merging consecutive updates to the same address locally before issuing a single atomic add on eviction. The tradeoff is additional shared memory usage. For kernels where contention is rare, the overhead may slightly increase latency, but the benefit grows superlinearly with contention rate, making this transformation highly effective for production workloads with moderate-to-high atomic traffic. Integrating a second GPU vendor into a previously NVIDIA- only fleet raised operational challenges in power, reliability, and observability. In addition to performance, operational maturity is just as critical to adopting a new vendor, and much of the tooling and fleet-management machinery we built generalizes to future accelerators. Reliability. We track MI350X on the same metrics as our NVIDIA fleet (training-failure rate, unplanned unavailability, and hardware-swap rate), and it is steadily converging toward NVIDIA-fleet levels: the training-failure rate has more than halved from its peak as we resolved new at-scale failure modes, namely HBM RAS, multi-chiplet memory-access faults, and silent data corruption (SDC). SDC, the bit-flips that corrupt re- sults without crashing, grows with cluster size and is the hardest to tame, so we address it end-to-end: a model-level checksum- and-replay check flags non-deterministic steps (distinguishing on-chip corruption from communication non-determinism); an all-pairs vote across a host’s GPUs pinpoints the culprit device (the one that disagrees with all its peers) and auto-quarantines the host; and the same check runs during host qualification and burn-in to screen SDC-prone hosts before they enter the training pool, catching over 2× the SDC of the vendor’s field-health tool. These mitigations preserve correctness: across the tens of models we train on both vendors, results reach numerical parity (training-loss difference within 0.3% and NE within ∼10−4 at multi-thousand-GPU scale).
