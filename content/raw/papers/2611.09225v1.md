## 摘要

Low-precision matrix throughput on modern GPUs is growing far faster than vector-arithmetic throughput. Attention therefore cannot realize the available GEMM performance because softmax has become the pipeline bottleneck. We introduce Fused-Softmax Attention, a training-free algorithm that reformulates and approximates softmax to fold its dominant operations into the attention GEMMs, moving work from vector units to matrix cores. Implemented for the AMD CDNA 4 architecture, our MXFP8, MXFP6 and MXFP4 kernels reach approximately 3.0, 4.0 and 4.6 PFLOP/s on an AMD Instinct MI355X GPU, yielding speedups of $2.1\times$, $2.8\times$ and $3.2\times$ over the BF16 attention. Importantly, the additional error introduced by Fused-Softmax remains below the error floor set by quantizing the GEMM operands. On Wan2.2-T2V-A14B, a hybrid MXFP6/4 schedule delivers up to $2.05\times$ end-to-end speedup while preserving the visual quality of the outputs. The kernels ship in AMD's open-source AITER library, and they powered AMD's MLPerf Inference v6.1 Wan2.2 submission, which ran $1.7\times$ faster than AMD's previous-round result on the same hardware. Our results show that redesigning softmax to utilize the matrix pipeline enables attention to capitalize on continued growth in low-precision matrix throughput.

## Abstract

Low-precision matrix throughput on modern GPUs is growing far faster than vector-arithmetic throughput. Attention therefore cannot realize the available GEMM performance because softmax has become the pipeline bottleneck. We introduce Fused-Softmax Attention, a training-free algorithm that reformulates and approximates softmax to fold its dominant operations into the attention GEMMs, moving work from vector units to matrix cores. Implemented for the AMD CDNA™4 architecture, our MXFP8, MXFP6 and MXFP4 kernels reach approximately 3.0, 4.0 and 4.6 PFLOP/s on an AMD Instinct™ MI355X GPU, yielding speedups of 2.1×, 2.8× and 3.2× over the BF16 attention. Importantly, the additional error introduced by Fused-Softmax remains below the error floor set by quantizing the GEMM operands. On Wan2.2-T2V-A14B, a hybrid MXFP6/4 schedule delivers up to 2.05× end-to-end speedup while preserving the visual quality of the outputs. The kernels ship in AMD’s open-source AITER library, and they powered AMD’s MLPerf® Inference v6.1 Wan2.2 submission, which ran 1.7× faster than AMD’s previous-round result on the same hardware. Our results show that redesigning softmax to utilize the matrix pipeline enables attention to capitalize on continued growth in low-precision matrix throughput.

## 1
Introduction

Transformer attention (Vaswani et al., 2017) is a cornerstone algorithm of modern AI models, but its quadratic complexity in terms of sequence length makes it a computational bottleneck in long-sequence settings. This is particularly relevant in video generation models (Team Wan et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2024; MiniMaxAI, 2026), where higher resolution and longer videos increase the effective sequence length. The FlashAttention family (Dao et al., 2022; Dao, 2024; Shah et al., 2024) has become the stan- dard approach to map the attention algorithm efficiently to modern GPU hardware. In its latest version, FlashAttention-4 (Zadouri et al., 2026), software pipelining has evolved to overlap the two kinds of work in attention, the matrix multiplies and the vector work of the softmax, exploiting independent execution units in modern datacenter GPUs. However, the throughput of these execution units has scaled asymmetrically across recent datacenter GPU generations, leaving vector throughput far behind and making the softmax the bottleneck. Related work addresses this bottleneck by reducing the cost and number of the softmax-related vector operations. FlashAttention-4 uses polynomial approximation for part of the exponentials and skips most output rescales. Its follow-ups (Zhang et al., 2026b; Deng et al., 2026) quantize the matrix operands to FP8 and below but leave the softmax unchanged. Two concurrent works, VC-Attention (Li et al., 2026) and Direct-P from FP4 FlashAttention-4 (Hu, 2026), replace the costly exponential with an affine transformation into the quantized probabilities. *Equal contribution. In contrast to these works, our proposed method moves most of the softmax work onto the matrix cores and uses a high-quality, in-kernel conversion path to produce quantized probabilities. Our Fused-Softmax pipeline combines the following key features: • Freezing the maximum speculatively, which moves the max subtraction into the score GEMM’s accumulator and removes the rescaling-related vector work. A gate on the running tile max detects overflow and triggers a rollback, which raises the frozen max and re-shifts the scores. • Exponential approximation using the Schraudolph method (Schraudolph, 1999), with the affine trans- formation folded into the score GEMM’s operands alongside the frozen maximum, off the vector unit. • A hardware-efficient two-stage conversion to quantized P: Stage-A performs the Schraudolph conversion, and Stage-B scales the result to the target format. The running tile max is used to derive the E8M0 P scales in the microscaling (MX) format (Open Compute Project, 2023). • The row sum is moved out of the vector path and done as an efficient matrix multiply using the quantized P. This leaves only a maximum reduction and two conversions on the vector unit, effectively unlocking the benefits of quantizing the matrix operands to FP8 and lower formats. We implement our pipeline on CDNA 4 and evaluate it on the AMD Instinct™MI355X GPU. Section 5.1 ablates the accuracy cost of each feature and the operand quantization it enables. We evaluate a progressive series of kernel variants from MXFP8 through MXFP6 to MXFP4. The MXFP8 configuration reaches 2978 TFLOP/s (2.07× BF16), at a cosine similarity of 0.9984. The MXFP6 attention reaches 3973 TFLOP/s (2.76× BF16), at 0.9979. The frontier MXFP4 attention reaches 4586 TFLOP/s (3.2× BF16), at 0.9787. The kernels are in production: they ship in AITER behind the mha v4 entrypoint (AMD, 2025), and powered AMD’s MLPerf® Inference v6.1 Wan2.2-T2V-A14B submission (Zhang et al., 2026a; Arunachalam et al., 2026), whose single-stream latency fell 1.7× from the previous round on the same MI355X system, with quantized attention the largest single contributor.

## 2
Background

In this section, we establish the background of our work. We review FlashAttention and the online softmax, introduce the asymmetric scaling of matrix and vector throughput that leaves the softmax behind, and survey the recent work addressing it. Throughout, uppercase symbols such as Q and S denote matrices, and lowercase symbols such as m and ℓdenote per-row quantities, which we treat as column vectors that broadcast across the columns of a matrix wherever the two are combined. A subscript on a matrix selects a tile of rows rather than an entry: Qi is the ith block of M consecutive rows of Q, Kj the jth block of N rows of K, and a double subscript Sij the M × N block of S formed from Qi and Kj. Row-wise reductions are written rowmax and rowsum, each returning one value per row. 2.1 FlashAttention Let Q, K, V ∈RNctx×d be the query, key and value matrices of one attention head, with Nctx the sequence length and d the head dimension. Attention computes S = α QK⊤, P = softmax(S), O = PV, (1) with α = 1/ d and the softmax taken row-wise. FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022; Dao, 2024; Shah et al., 2024) never materializes S or P in device memory. A workgroup owns a single query tile Qi ∈RM×d of M rows and loops over the keys and values Kj, Vj ∈RN×d in tiles of N rows, indexed j = 0, . . . , J −1 with J = ⌈Nctx/N⌉. Iteration j computes the score tile Sij = α QiK⊤ j and folds it into the running state by online softmax (Milakov and Gimelshein, 2018; Rabe and Staats, 2021): Xij = (log2 e) Sij, (2) mj = max(mj−1, rowmax(Xij)) , (3) Pij = 2Xij−mj, rj = 2mj−1−mj, (4) ℓj = rj ℓj−1 + rowsum(Pij), (5) Oj = rj Oj−1 + PijVj. (6) The recurrence starts from m−1 = −∞and ℓ−1 = O−1 = 0, which makes the first rescaling factor r0 vanish and leaves ℓ0 and O0 equal to the contribution of tile 0 alone. Once the loop ends, the output accumulator is normalized by 1/ℓJ−1 to complete the softmax. Equation (2) scales the score by the constant log2 e, as FlashAttention implementations commonly do to use the hardware’s base-two exponential; in this paper the choice carries more weight. It replaces ex by 2x everywhere downstream and leaves the probabilities untouched, since 2Xij = eSij exactly. Base two is the base the hardware already works in: it is the base of the exponent field of a floating-point number, of the hardware exponential instruction, and of the Schraudolph construction of Section 4.3. Carrying the softmax in base two is what later allows the exponential to be absorbed into a format conversion rather than evaluated. Subtracting the row maximum before the exponential keeps the exponent and the output accumulator inside the FP32 range, but it is also what couples the iterations: whenever the maximum grows, the accumulated output and the running row sum computed under the old maximum have to be corrected by rj. Each iteration therefore contains two GEMMs, QiK⊤ j and PijVj, separated by a body of vector work: the row maximum, the max subtraction, the exponential, the row sum, and the rescaling of O and ℓby rj. 2.2 Asymmetric scaling of matrix and vector throughput Matrix and vector throughput have not been scaling the same way. As Zadouri et al. (2026) note, successive accelerator generations have spent their power and silicon-area budget on the matrix-multiply units, the components that dominate performance, while the vector units have stayed close to flat. Attention runs into this directly, because the softmax sitting between its two GEMMs is vector work and does not benefit. We quantify the gap on CDNA, and then show that it is not a quirk of CDNA. Table 1 gives matrix and vector throughput on the last two CDNA generations, per clock per compute unit (CU), taken from the official CDNA 4 whitepaper (Advanced Micro Devices, Inc., 2025). The matrix core has gained along two independent axes, one generational and the other from the operand format, so a kernel moving from BF16 on CDNA 3 to MXFP6 on CDNA 4 has 8× the matrix throughput. Peak VALU throughput, meanwhile, has stayed flat at 128 elements per clock per CU. Part of the expo- nentials is emulated on the vector units, which on NVIDIA hardware run alongside a separate exponential unit: the integer part is evaluated exactly with the Schraudolph construction (Schraudolph, 1999), and a third-degree polynomial approximates the fractional part. FlashAttention-4 targets BF16 attention. We use the same Schraudolph construction, but with a linear fractional part: we find its error small next to that of quantizing the attention inputs (Section 5.1), and the transformation being affine, it can be folded into the score GEMM operands (Section 4.3). For the output rescaling, FlashAttention-4 keeps a stale row maximum and rescales the output only when the running maximum exceeds it by more than a threshold set to ensure numerical stability; otherwise the stale row maximum is used to shift the scores and the output rescaling can be omitted. Our frozen maximum (Section 4.1) achieves the same skipping of the output rescaling, but more importantly, moving the frozen maximum subtraction speculatively before the score GEMM is what enables our affine Schraudolph transformation to be fused to the score GEMM (Section 4.3). The rollback scheme (Section 4.1) is what ensures the numerical stability for us. Ping-pong pipelining, introduced for attention by FlashAttention-3 (Shah et al., 2024) and kept by FlashAttention-4, phase-shifts two groups of warps so that one runs its GEMMs while the other runs its softmax. The producer/consumer split FlashAttention-3 pairs with it does not carry over to CDNA, where registers are allocated statically and a producer wave would hold a full register budget while doing no arithmetic. HipKittens (Hu et al., 2026) replaces it with an eight-wave ping-pong, which we adopt (Figure 1): a workgroup spans the four single-instruction multiple-data (SIMD) units of a CU, waves 0–3 and 4–7 form two groups that share a SIMD pairwise, and a barrier holds a phase offset so one group’s vector instructions issue while the other’s matrix fused multiply–add (MFMA) instructions are in flight. The SageAttention series (Zhang et al., 2025c;a;b) quantizes the attention GEMMs progressively from INT8 to INT4 for QK and FP8 to FP4 for PV . Several follow-ups to FlashAttention-4 quantize its GEMMs to FP8 and below. Attn-QAT (Zhang et al., 2026b) quantizes QK to FP8 or FP4 and PV to FP8, and recovers the accuracy lost at 4 bits by fine-tuning the model with quantization-aware training. Direct-P (Hu, 2026) maps the scores with a fitted linear function into MXFP4 probabilities, which relies heavily on that format having only a few representable levels. VC-Attention (Li et al., 2026) evaluates the exponential with Schraudolph’s construction (Schraudolph, 1999) and reads the result as an FP8 probability in one conversion. That single conversion to an 8-bit integer covers only a narrow range of the exponential; Sections 4.2 and 5.1 find the same 8-bit proxy too costly in accuracy in our pipeline. ## 4
Fused-Softmax Attention

Fused-Softmax Attention removes most of the softmax’s vector work from the steady-state loop by folding it into the two GEMMs and a format conversion. The maximum is frozen speculatively and subtracted inside the score GEMM, so the output and the row sum are never rescaled unless a later tile overflows, in which case an exact rollback restores them. The exponential is approximated by Schraudolph’s affine map, carried by the score GEMM’s MX scale and the subtraction, and so reduces to a conversion; a second conversion quantizes P, and the block maxima computed along the way supply both the overflow gate and the MX scales of P. The row sum leaves the vector unit as well, evaluated as a matrix multiply over the quantized probabilities. In the steady state the vector unit runs only the block-maximum reduction and the two conversions. We adopt the eight-wave ping-pong of Section 2.3, the CDNA form of the pipeline FlashAttention-3 introduced and FlashAttention-4 kept, and fuse the softmax into it. Data movement uses the same two-group split: the groups cooperatively prefetch K and V into free LDS slots while computing on the tiles already resident in the others (Figure 2). Waves 0–3 fetch K and waves 4–7 fetch V , each one tile ahead into a two-slot ring, so a transfer for the next tile is in flight for the whole of the current one, and a barrier publishes each slot before it is read. Schraudolph approximation. Schraudolph’s observation (Schraudolph, 1999) is that a float stores a biased exponent and a mantissa in consecutive fields, so a power of two can be built as a bit pattern instead of computed. For a score S in natural-log units, P ≈read as float (Cbits · S + Dbits).to(uint) where a target format of M mantissa bits and E exponent bits, of bias b = 2E−1 −1, takes Cbits = 2M log2 e and Dbits = 2M(b −ϵ). The exponent field receives the integer part of S log2 e and the mantissa field the fractional part, interpolated linearly over each doubling interval,1 while the true function varies exponentially; ϵ ≈0.05798 is a tuned value that minimizes the root-mean-square (RMS) interpolation error. The exponential is therefore one FMA and one cast to an unsigned integer whose bits are read as the target floating-point format; this cast is Stage-A. Choosing the proxy width. QK leaves the scores in FP32, and Stage-A can cast them to an unsigned integer of any width, the Schraudolph proxy, whose bits are then read as a float of the same width and narrowed by Stage-B. There are three options: • U32, read as FP32 and then scaled to the P format. This is the naive route, but the 32-bit conversion has no packed form, so it misses the packed conversion family and is slow. • U16, read as BF16 and then scaled to the P format. Both stages have packed instructions, so this route is fast. BF16 keeps the eight exponent bits of FP32, so the dynamic range of P available to the rollback gate is unchanged, and its seven mantissa bits give up little accuracy. • U8, read directly as E4M3, in a single stage. It has no packed form either, so it is no faster than the two-stage U16 route, and its three mantissa bits and four exponent bits cost too much accuracy and range. The accuracy cost of the U16 proxy is small. The Schraudolph approximation has an intrinsic 1.770% RMS relative error that no width removes, and U16 (M = 7) raises that only to 1.777%, whereas U8 (M = 3) raises the RMS error to 3.141%. Section 5.1 measures the effect of each proxy on the attention output, and Appendix B quantifies the accuracy of each proxy width. Dividing through by 216 gives the affine coordinate a = C(X −m) + D = C X + β, C = 2−9, D = 2−9(127 −ϵ), β = D −C m, and 65535a then approximates the pattern above, the factor 65535/65536 accounting for the small normal- ization difference. The additional factor C is a power of two, so it can be folded into the resident Q MX exponents by subtracting nine. The speculative reference contributes the per-row bias β. A BF16 MFMA broadcasts this bias into the accumulator seed, and the microscaled QK MFMA adds the scaled product. Its output is therefore a directly. ## 6
Conclusion

We presented Fused-Softmax Attention, a quantized attention pipeline that addresses the growing imbalance between matrix-core and vector-unit throughput. A speculatively frozen row maximum and an affine exponential approximation make max subtraction and exponential evaluation foldable into the score GEMM. A two-stage conversion then produces microscaled probabilities, reusing its block reduction to derive their scales and detect overflow. Detected overshoots trigger an accurate rollback, while evaluating the row sum as a matrix multiplication—sparse for MXFP8—removes the remaining reduction from the vector path. Implemented for the AMD CDNA™4 architecture, the resulting MXFP8, MXFP6 and MXFP4 kernels reach approximately 3.0, 4.0 and 4.6 PFLOP/s on an AMD Instinct™MI355X GPU, yielding speedups of 2.1×, 2.8× and 3.2× over BF16 attention. The additional error from Fused-Softmax remains below the error floor set by quantizing the GEMM operands. In the cross-platform comparison with published related methods, our reported throughput is 1.26–1.56× higher at similar or higher cosine similarity. These kernel gains translate to end-to-end diffusion inference. A hybrid MXFP6/4 schedule delivers up to 2.1× speedup on video generation while preserving the visual quality of the outputs. Evaluations on Wan2.2-T2V-A14B, HunyuanVideo and MiniMax-H3 show a consistent precision-quality pattern: MXFP8 and MXFP6 stay close to BF16, pure MXFP4 maximizes throughput at a visible quality cost, and the hybrid schedule recovers that loss. The kernels are deployed: they ship in the open-source AITER library behind the mha v4 entry point (AMD, 2025), where they compose with block-sparse attention. An earlier version of the hybrid kernel powered AMD’s MLPerf® Inference v6.1 Wan2.2-T2V-A14B submission (Zhang et al., 2026a; Arunachalam et al., 2026), whose SingleStream latency fell 1.7× from the previous round on the same MI355X system. Overall, our work shows that recasting softmax in terms of matrix operations enables attention to capitalize on growing low-precision matrix throughput.

VBench evaluation

We follow the benchmarking protocol of the MLPerf® Inference v6.1 text-to-video benchmark for Wan-2.2- T2V-A14B (Reddi et al., 2020; MLCommons Association, 2026). It generates 720p videos (81 frames) in 20 denoising steps with the UniPC scheduler (Zhao et al., 2023) and scores them with VBench. Queries are issued by the open-source MLPerf® LoadGen library, in the Offline scenario for the VBench runs and the SingleStream scenario for the latency measurement (Section 5.3.3). We generate the 248 prompts of the VBench set (Huang et al., 2024) with Wan2.2-T2V-A14B (81 frames at 1280×720, 20 sampling steps, the reference guidance of 4 for the high-noise expert and 3 for the low-noise expert, the reference negative prompt, one fixed seed and initial latent), once with BF16 attention and once with each of our quantized kernels and the hybrid schedule, which runs MXFP6 attention for the first and last three of the 20 denoising steps and MXFP4 for the fourteen between them. The self-attention calls have s=75600, h=40 and d=128. We run the BF16 Diffusers (von Platen et al., 2022) checkpoint of the model through xDiT (Fang et al., 2024) with torch.compile (Ansel et al., 2024). GEMMs stay in BF16 and cross-attention uses the BF16 attention kernel of AITER (AMD, 2025), the same kernel as the BF16 self-attention baseline, so only the self-attention kernel changes between the runs. Flow shift. ## B
Proxy and Schraudolph accuracy

Section 4.2 narrows the Schraudolph proxy from the classic 32-bit FP32 pattern to a 16-bit BF16 pattern. This appendix measures what the narrower mantissa costs in accuracy, and Table 8 sets that cost against the reach each candidate format affords. The Schraudolph approximation emulates the exponential operation by exponentiating the exponent bits while linearly interpolating the mantissa bits. For emulating 2x, there are two main error sources: • linear ramp: the linear interpolation of mantissas versus exponentiating the mantissas. • pattern quantization: representing the interpolated result in the corresponding floating-point format by rounding. When it comes to the choice of proxy, the linear ramp is proxy-invariant: it is inherent to the Schraudolph approximation. Pattern quantization, by contrast, depends on the mantissa width of the datatype in question. Figure 7 illustrates the linear ramp error over the range of input x. Letting f denote the fractional part of x −ϵ, an M-bit mantissa field cannot hold f itself, only ˆf = round(f 2M)/2M, so the pattern carries ˆf where the argument still calls for f. At M = 10 it is 0.049%, lost beneath the ramp’s 1.770%; at M = 7 it is 0.391%, adding 0.162% in quadrature for a 0.4% relative rise in RMS; at M = 3 it reaches 6.250% and dominates, lifting RMS by 77%. P’s destination is FP6, whose own three mantissa bits carry the same 6.250% half-step, so the BF16 proxy is 16× finer than the format it feeds, and its rounding vanishes beneath stage B’s. Hence the design rule: the proxy needs mantissa bits comfortably above the destination’s, and 7 against 3 leaves four bits of headroom, where an E4M3 proxy would carry exactly three and quantize twice at the same granularity. Therefore, the efficient choice is BF16. C Evaluation on the 16-bit maximum operations Section 4.2 mentions placing the block maximum after Stage-A, on packed data. Two 16-bit reductions can do that. v pk max u16 reads the codes as the unsigned integers they are and is exact; v pk maximum3 f16 retires twice as many values per issue but hands a BF16 pattern to an FP16 interpretation, and two properties of the construction decide whether a gate built on it is sound. Our MXFP6 kernel takes the exact reduction throughout; the MXFP4 one takes maximum3 in the steady state and falls back to the unsigned reduction in the rollback. The unsigned reduction is exact over every doubling. The packed normalized conversion is monotone in its affine input, so unsigned code order preserves input order across the whole code space: no peak can be dropped, and there is no Inf/NaN band to invent one. The pattern domain also makes the gate and the rollback integer work, one doubling being exactly 128 pattern units. Its only ceiling belongs to the conversion rather than the reduction, since a coordinate saturated at 0xFFFF is unrecoverable from its code. The price is issue slots: 15 two-input maxima and a halfword fold per 32-element block, 17 instructions against the 10 of a maximum3 chain. Stage-A cannot emit anything but a pattern. The packed normalized conversion clamps to [0, 1] before scaling by 65535, so the code written out always lies in [0, 65535]; a coordinate past the top saturates at 0xFFFF rather than wrapping to a small one. Below the sign bit, ordering survives the reinterpretation: FP16’s 1+5+10 split ranks positive codes exactly as BF16’s 1+8+7 does. What FP16 adds is an Inf/NaN band above 0x7C00, and it costs nothing, since the instruction propagates NaN and every FP16 NaN pattern is at least 0x7C00 unsigned, hence above any useful cap. The one unsigned compare that catches a genuine overshoot therefore also catches a poisoned reduction, after which the rollback re-reduces with v pk max u16. A large overshoot reaches the sign bit. Schraudolph writes the score into the exponent field, so an overshoot climbs through the exponent into bit 15 rather than saturating. Both formats read bit 15 as a sign but place their Inf/NaN bands at different heights, which opens a gap. Of the four bands in Table 9 only the third misreads: a pattern there is an ordinary negative finite to FP16, so maximum3 has nothing to propagate and returns the positive neighbor, and the peak leaves the reduction before the gate sees it. −∞(0xFC00) belongs to that band, since only NaN propagation is sign-agnostic. What matters is that the band is Inf- and NaN-free when interpreted as BF16. Stage-B reads the dropped pattern as BF16, where bit 15 is again a sign and the exponent sits far below the reference, so the row’s dominant probability is packed as a small negative weight: the mass is lost and the PV MFMA and the ℓsum take a finite perturbation of one row, with nothing to propagate. A NaN reaching those accumulators would destroy the output irrecoverably, and that is the failure this band cannot produce. A missed gate would instead cause a finite perturbation; we observed no such missed-gate failure across the evaluated model calls. The band is also hard to reach, opening at ∆= 129 against a cap at ∆= 32 and only when the peak is isolated within its own 32-element block — any block-mate above the cap but below 129 still fires the gate. maximum3 therefore guarantees 97 doublings rather than the whole code space, which is why MXFP6 pays the seven extra issues per block and only MXFP4 takes the cheaper reduction.

## D
Memory bandwidth roofline

The operands have to reach the matrix cores before they can be multiplied, over two hops: high-bandwidth memory (HBM) into LDS, and LDS into registers. We cost each against the matrix and vector work of Section 3. HBM to LDS. Without cache reuse, each Kj/Vj loads 2Nd, 1.5Nd, or Nd bytes at FP8, FP6, or FP4 through the HBM. Sharing 8 TB/s across 256 CUs at 2.4 GHz gives 13.02 B/clk/CU, hence (T FP8 HBM, T FP6 HBM, T FP4 HBM) ≈(2517, 1887, 1258) clk. We double-buffer LDS: slot 0 supplies registers for the current tile while slot 1 receives the next tile, then their roles swap. Both wave groups use the same Kj/Vj, so the next transfer arrives in time when THBM < TGEMM + TVALU. Even without cache reuse, these transfer costs remain below the combined matrix and vector work, and any reuse lowers them further. LDS to registers. Each of the eight waves reads Kj/Vj from LDS. At 256 B/clk/CU (Advanced Micro Devices, Inc., 2025), the ideal read costs are 8(2Nd, 1.5Nd, Nd) 256 = (1024, 768, 512) clk for FP8, FP6, and FP4, respectively. These are also below the matrix cycle cost, and we use this to our advantage by interleaving the LDS reading into the matrix execution shadow.
