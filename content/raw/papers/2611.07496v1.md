## 摘要

Attention is a core building block of frontier models and often dominates compute-bound prefill at long context. FP8 matrix instructions offer twice the peak throughput of BF16, but they accelerate only matrix multiplication, and the softmax between the two matrix multiplications of attention runs on vector units that FP8 does not accelerate. On the AMD Instinct MI355X, the vendor's AITER kernels reach 39% of peak FP8 matrix throughput at 32K tokens, compared with 49% in BF16. Attention is also more numerically sensitive to quantization than feed-forward layers, and unlike NVIDIA's Hopper and Blackwell GPUs, the MI355X does not track the completion of matrix instructions in hardware, so overlapping the softmax with the matrix multiplications depends on instruction order. We present ZYFA, prefill kernels for the MI355X built on three techniques: (1) computing each FP8 probability directly from its score with a multiply-add and an integer conversion instead of an exponential; (2) a ping-pong schedule in which one group of threads computes its softmax while another executes its matrix multiplications; and (3) scaling each block of probabilities up by a power of two before rounding, and normalizing by the sum of the rounded probabilities. On attention inputs captured from three models and timed at sustained clock speeds, ZYFA achieves 1.16-1.76x the throughput of AITER. On the Gaussian inputs of kernel benchmarks at 32K tokens, it achieves 1.26-1.37x at head dimension 128 and 1.99x for DeepSeek-style multi-head latent attention. The sustained clock of the MI355X depends on operand values, so throughput on random inputs does not reliably predict throughput on real model activations. At head dimension 128, ZYFA's numerical error is within a few percent of that of Composable Kernel and Triton and 62-78% lower than that of AITER's. The source code is available at https://github.com/Zyphra/zyfa.

At these lengths attention often dominates the prefill phase of autoregressive inference. IO-aware algorithms like FlashAttention perform the same arithmetic as standard attention, but stream blocks of keys and values through on-chip memory and keep a running softmax for each query, so that the score matrix never reaches the comparatively slow high- bandwidth memory (HBM) of the GPU [Dao et al., 2022, Dao, 2024]. The speed of attention then depends on how well its GPU kernel can keep the arithmetic units of the GPU busy, and because the throughput of matrix or tensor cores has increased significantly faster in subsequent GPU generations than all other hardware elements, the kernel engineer’s primary focus has shifted to keeping them fed. FlashAttention-3 hides the softmax behind asynchronous matrix instructions on Hopper GPUs [Shah et al., 2024], and FlashAttention-4 approximates portions of the exponentials with polynomials on Blackwell GPUs, whose matrix hardware units grew faster than their exponential units [Zadouri et al., 2026]. Low precision worsens this hardware gap further. Recent GPUs execute 8-bit floating- point (FP8) matrix instructions at twice the theoretical BF16 rate [Micikevicius et al., 2022, Advanced Micro Devices, 2025b], so FP8 attention could, in principle, take half the time of BF16 attention if it were purely bound by its matrix multiplications. Abstract—Attention is a core building block of frontier models and often dominates compute-bound prefill at long context. FP8 matrix instructions offer twice the peak throughput of BF16, but they accelerate only matrix multiplication, so a GPU kernel’s total speedup from FP8 shrinks as other operations take a larger share of the overall kernel time. For example in attention, the softmax between the two matrix multiplications is on vector units which FP8 does not accelerate. On the AMD Instinct MI355X, the vendor’s AITER kernels (AMD’s library of inference kernels) reach 39% of peak FP8 matrix throughput at 32K tokens, compared with 49% in BF16. Unlike NVIDIA’s Hopper and Blackwell GPUs, the MI355X also does not track the completion of matrix instructions in hardware, so fine-grained instruction order must be carried out via more manual methods like assembly instruction ordering, which is vital for attention kernels such as overlapping the softmax with the matrix multiplications. Finally, attention is more numerically sensitive to quantization than feed-forward layers, so considerable numerical analysis must be done to ensure fidelity. To overcome these challenges, we present ZYFA, prefill kernels for the MI355X built on three techniques: (1) computing each FP8 probability directly from its score with a multiply-add and an integer conversion instead of an exponential, since an FP8 bit pattern approximates a scaled base-2 logarithm of its raw value; (2) a ping-pong schedule in which one group of threads computes its softmax while another executes its matrix multiplications; and (3) scaling each block of probabilities up by a power of two before rounding, so that far fewer small probabilities round to zero, and normalizing by the sum of the rounded probabilities, so that the attention weights still sum to one. On attention inputs captured from three representative models and timed at sustained clock speeds (the lower, steady clock that the GPU settles at after a few seconds of back-to-back launches), ZYFA achieves 1.16–1.76× the throughput of AITER. On the random Gaussian inputs of kernel benchmarks at 32K tokens, ZYFA achieves 1.26–1.37× the throughput of AITER at head dimension 128 and 1.99× for DeepSeek-style multi-head latent attention. The sustained clock of the MI355X depends on operand values, so throughput on random inputs does not reliably predict throughput on real model activations, where ZYFA takes fewer clock cycles than AITER’s kernel but runs at a lower per-cycle clock speed. At head dimension 128, ZYFA’s numerical error is within a few percent of that of AMD’s Composable Kernel and Triton kernels and 62–78% lower than that of AITER’s. The source code is available at https://github.com/Zyphra/zyfa. However, on the AMD Instinct MI355X, FP8 attention gains much less than this in practice. At 32K tokens on random Gaussian inputs, the FP8 attention kernel of AITER, AMD’s library of inference kernels [AMD, 2025a], reaches 39% of the FP8 datasheet peak, while its BF16 kernel reaches 49% of the BF16 peak. Accuracy is a second concern. DeepSeek-V3, for example, trains its linear layers, including the experts, in FP8 but keeps attention in higher precision because attention is more numerically sensitive [DeepSeek-AI, 2024b]. This paper asks what limits the FP8 attention operation on the MI355X GPU, how to work around these limits, and how the error of FP8 attention can be sourced and mitigated.

Read as an integer, the 8-bit bit pattern (or code) of a positive e4m3 number, the FP8 format with 4 exponent and 3 mantissa bits, grows by 8 each time the value doubles, so it is close in effect to a scaled and shifted base-2 logarithm of the value being represented. The code of a probability p = 2x, where x is the score minus the row maximum in base-2 units, is therefore close to 8x plus a constant, the same relation that Schraudolph’s fast exponential uses for IEEE floating-point numbers [Schraudolph, 1999]. At head dimension 128 the FP8 kernel computes this code with one multiply-add and one integer conversion, which we call lean P. Lean P replaces both the exponential and the floating-point conversion, and lowers the vector cycles per block from 896 to 384. It is an approximation that raises the output error of one attention call to 1.22–1.36× that of exact P, which evaluates the exponential, but the kernel’s error stays below that of AITER’s kernel, at 0.26× in geometric mean (Section VI-B). Essentially, lean P trades a small amount of numerical accuracy for speed. Lean P is optional, and ZYFA uses it by default at head dimension 128. The kernel at the MLA shape keeps exact P only because there lean P falls just below the signal-to-noise threshold of our correctness test in 1 of 24 configurations, although it matches exact P end to end (Section VI-B). units whose speed is the same in both FP8 and BF16. For 32 queries against a block of 128 keys at head dimension 128, the two matrix products take 2048 matrix-unit cycles in BF16 and 1024 in FP8, while the softmax takes 896 vector-unit cycles in FP8, even more than the 768 it takes in BF16, because converting a probability to FP8 costs more than converting it to BF16 (see Section III-B). Having nearly the same clock cycles spent in vector units and matrix units is troubling for the programmer, since one must overlap the softmax with matrix products in order to keep the matrix cores busy. FP8 therefore requires almost perfect overlap (1024 matrix cycles against 896 vector cycles), but BF16 has considerable slack (2048 against 768) that makes implementation easier. In our thread traces of AITER’s FP8 kernel (cycle-by-cycle records of every instruction that a wave issues), we see the matrix unit idle for 31.7% of cycles while the softmax runs. NVIDIA’s Hopper and Blackwell GPUs provide asynchronous matrix instructions whose completion events are tracked by hardware [Shah et al., 2024, Zadouri et al., 2026], so the hardware tells a kernel when a matrix product is ready and overlap is much easier to organize by the kernel programmer. On the MI355X, however, matrix instructions keep running while the wave that issued them (a group of 64 threads that issue instructions together) issues other instructions, and the hardware does not track their completion, so the overlap depends entirely on the order in which the kernel issues its instructions, whose order must also ensure that each matrix instruction has finished before its result is read (Section II-A). We find this often necessitates raw GPU assembly kernels, whose development can be eased by generator functions written in higher-level languages like Python. Two further changes keep the FP8 probabilities as accurate as possible. In a long row of the attention matrix most probabilities lie far below the largest one, often below 2−6, where e4m3 values lose precision, or below 2−10, where they underflow to zero and we lose information. The kernels therefore multiply each block of 64 probabilities by a power of two so that its largest value is close to 1, and the scaled FP8 matrix instruction of CDNA4 undoes the factor at no cost. ## II. BACKGROUND

A. The MI355X GPU The AMD Instinct MI355X (CDNA4 architecture) has 256 compute units (CUs), analogous to the streaming mul- tiprocessors of NVIDIA GPUs. The CUs are distributed across eight chiplets (XCDs), each with its own 4 MB L2 cache. At the maximum shader clock (SCLK), 2.4 GHz, the datasheet peak dense matrix throughput is 2.5 PFLOP/s in BF16 and 5.0 PFLOP/s in FP8 [Advanced Micro Devices, 2025b] (Table I). Each CU has four SIMD units, which issue the instructions of the threads that run on them, and 160 KB of shared memory called the local data share (LDS). Threads run in groups of 64 called waves, analogous to the 32-thread warps of NVIDIA GPUs, and we call the position of a thread within its wave its lane. Waves in the same workgroup, a group of waves launched together like a thread block on NVIDIA GPUs, run on one CU and share LDS and workgroup barriers. A wave stays on one SIMD and issues its instructions in program order. Each SIMD has a register file and two execution units, which we call pipes, a vector pipe for elementwise arithmetic (VALU) and a matrix pipe for matrix fused multiply-add (MFMA) B. Wave 4 A Python generator emits the FP8 kernels as CDNA4 assembly, one kernel per head dimension. The generator gives us the fine-grained control of assembly together with the ease of development of Python. The two waves of each SIMD take turns in two phases per 128-key block, with a workgroup barrier after each phase (Figure 6). FlashAttention-3 calls this arrangement ping-pong scheduling [Shah et al., 2024], HipKittens uses it on CDNA GPUs [Hu et al., 2025], and AITER uses it as well (Section III-B). In each phase one wave, the MFMA wave, issues the matrix instructions of its block. These instructions are two row-sum MFMAs, eight QK⊤MFMAs for the next block, and eight PV MFMAs for the current block. Between these MFMAs the MFMA wave computes the row maximum and the rescale of the next block. The other wave, the softmax wave, computes the FP8 probabilities. In the next phase the two waves swap roles, so the matrix pipe always has one wave that feeds it. The wave that reaches the barrier last sets the length of a phase, and we call it the critical wave.

The inputs, which we call the Gaussian inputs, are q, k, and v drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation 0.1, which makes the attention probabilities of each query row nearly uniform (Section VI-A). The GPU idles for 5 s before each configuration with S ≥16K. Each configuration runs at least three times, the order of kernels rotates between repetitions, and the reported spread is the range over repetitions. Throughput is F from Equation 1, which counts the FLOPs of QK⊤and PV in the causal half of the score matrix, divided by the time. E. End-to-End Accuracy For end-to-end accuracy, we run each model in BF16 and replace its attention, per layer and per head, by the kernel under test. The study uses Mistral-7B-v0.3 (dqk=128, 32 query and 8 KV heads), Qwen3-8B (dqk=128, 32 query and 8 KV heads, with QK normalization), and DeepSeek-V2-Lite (MLA with dqk=192, dv=128, and 16 heads), all with BF16 weights. The documents come from the PG-19 test books [Rae et al., 2020] in a seeded shuffle. A document is the text of one book after its first 2000 characters, cut to 8K, 16K, or 32K tokens. Each model is evaluated with teacher forcing (the model reads the true text, and we score its prediction of each next token) on 48 documents from 48 books, one third at each length. A separate calibration set of 12 documents from 12 other books, one third at each length, is used to select policies and to set static FP8 scales. C. Sustained Timing, Clock, and Power Kernel-only timing on the Gaussian inputs of kernel bench- marks measures a kernel at the clock that a short burst of launches gets. Because the clock depends on the data and settles under sustained load (Section III-C), we also time kernels on model activations in long runs. We capture the post-RoPE q, k, and v (after the rotary position embedding, as the attention kernel receives them) of one layer each of Mistral- 7B-v0.3 [Jiang et al., 2023], Qwen3-8B [Qwen Team, 2025], and DeepSeek-V2-Lite [DeepSeek-AI, 2024a] on WikiText- 2 [Merity et al., 2017] at S=16K. Timing at sustained clocks means timing only after this clock has settled. In a sustained run one kernel launches back to back on a GPU that runs nothing else. ## VIII. DISCUSSION

Lean P and the number format: Lean P, ZYFA’s com- putation of each FP8 probability with one multiply-add and an integer conversion instead of an exponential, replaces the evaluation of a function with a property of the number format. A floating-point code already stores the base-2 logarithm of its value in its exponent bits and interpolates linearly between powers of two in its mantissa bits, so the code of 2x needs only the linear map of Equation 2 and a rounding to an integer. FlashAttention-4 also avoids the hardware exponential, but it evaluates a polynomial in FP32 and still converts the result to the output format [Zadouri et al., 2026]. Lean P skips both steps, because the output format itself is the approximation. Its error is therefore set by the coarseness of the format. After rounding, lean P is within 7.5% of p, against 5.9% for exact P, which rounds the exact 2x to e4m3, and in full models this difference is small next to the error of quantizing Q, K, and V (Section VI-C). The same construction applies to any format whose code grows by a fixed step each time its value doubles, which includes e5m2 and, with coarser steps, FP4, although we have not tested these formats. Remaining limits of FP8 attention on the MI355X: At 32K tokens on the Gaussian inputs of kernel benchmarks, ZYFA’s FP8 kernel at dqk=128 reaches 2672 TFLOP/s, 53% of the datasheet peak and 88% of a synthetic loop with its instruction mix. Three gaps remain, and each points to a different improvement. The first is the clock. Under sustained load, after seconds of back-to-back launches, the kernel runs at about 1628 MHz on captured activations (the q, k, and v of real model layers) at 16K tokens, against the 2.4 GHz that the datasheet peak assumes, and even a loop of FP8 MFMAs alone settles at 2.07 GHz. Most of the distance to the datasheet peak therefore comes from the limit on current that sets the clock (Section VI-A) rather than from the schedule, and kernels that switch fewer bits per FLOP could gain directly. The second is the transitions between query tiles. The matrix pipe is busy for 86% of the cycles in steady state, in the middle of the key loop of one query tile, but for 78% over the whole key loop, so overlapping the end of one tile with the start of the next could recover much of this difference. The third is the work that remains on the MFMA wave, the one of the two waves on a SIMD that issues the matrix instructions in a phase and, with lean P, sets the length of the phase. That wave also computes the row maximum and the rescale, so layers that rescale often take longer (Appendix A), and a schedule that moves this work to the softmax wave, which computes the probabilities, would make the kernel less sensitive to the data. where matrix throughput grew faster than the throughput of the exponential unit. ## IX. CONCLUSION

On the MI355X, FP8 attention is limited by the vector work of the softmax and by its clock speed, which depends on the input data. By writing each FP8 probability directly from its score and hiding the softmax behind matrix instructions, ZYFA’s FP8 kernels reach 1.16–1.76× the throughput of the default kernels of AITER, AMD’s library of inference kernels, on activations captured from real models and timed at sustained clocks, after seconds of back-to-back launches. In full models most of the FP8 error comes from quantizing the inputs and is spread over most heads, so keeping the most sensitive heads in BF16 is not a useful middle ground, while a Hadamard rotation of Q and K roughly halves the error from quantizing the inputs at almost no cost. Recommendations: GPU kernel developers, especially those working on numerically sensitive operations like quan- tized model blocks, should develop with real model activations and at sustained clocks, the lower steady clock that the GPU reaches after seconds of back-to-back launches, because the clock depends on data distribution and its rate of change. To lower quantization error, target the quantization of the inputs, for example with a Hadamard rotation of Q and K and K smoothing, which cost one extra pass over Q and K. Because V adds the least input error, keeping QK⊤in BF16 and running only PV in FP8 would remove most of the input error for three quarters of the matrix cycles of BF16. Reduce the vector work per score before adding matrix throughput. A timing proxy that issues the QK⊤MFMAs in FP4 and halves the LDS reads of K raises the throughput by at most 10% at 32K. Keep packed FP32 instructions out of a softmax that runs next to MFMAs. Use head dimensions of at least 128, because the matrix cycles per block grow with dqk+dv while the softmax cycles do not, so at head dimension 64 even lean P keeps the vector pipe about as busy as the matrix pipe.

Small differences between layers: Every layer of a model computes attention of the same shape, so in every layer each kernel issues the same instructions and moves the same bytes. Only the data differ, and the data can change the time of a kernel in two ways. They set how often the running maximum of a row grows, which decides how often the kernel rescales its output (Section IV-A), and they set how much the values in the arithmetic units change from one instruction to the next, which decides the clock (Section III-C). In most layers both effects are small, so the speedup is close to a constant of the model.
